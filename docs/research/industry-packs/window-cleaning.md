## Executive summary

**Observed evidence:** Window-cleaning businesses include recurring residential rounds, storefront/commercial route accounts, and quoted one-off or high-access work. Residential and small storefront accounts benefit from route density and property-specific access notes; commercial sites may constrain service windows around customer traffic. Rain policy is not uniform: operators in a Canadian forum debated moving all work, completing interiors, or offering limited touch-ups. OSHA's general-industry rule includes window-cleaner positioning-system requirements; IWCA provides route/residential and rope-descent safety training paths.

**Inference:** A strong pack should treat the property and its window-service scope as durable context, while pane/surface counts, interior/exterior selection, access method, and condition belong to a quote or visit. Safety-sensitive high-rise work requires more documentation and skill/equipment checks than ground-level route cleaning. The profile does not support making every company a high-rise contractor or imposing one weather/cancellation policy.

## Business model and customer segments

Residential homeowners buy exterior-only or interior/exterior cleans, often recurring; storefronts and small businesses may buy monthly route visits; property managers, offices, and larger commercial buildings may procure scheduled service or tendered work. Customers value clean glass, convenient scheduling, and property care. Operators describe recurring local rounds and customer access/time-window expectations. One-time construction cleanup, hard-water removal, screen/gutter add-ons, and rope-access high-rise service change quoting, safety, crew, and equipment needs. Solo operators may keep a small round manually; teams assign routes and manage customer messages. Geography and route density matter because a weather-driven reschedule across a large territory can disrupt several days.

## Terminology and durable records

Use **customer**, **property/site**, **round/route**, **clean/visit**, **pane/surface**, **screen**, **track/sill**, **crew/technician**, **quote**, and **touch-up/callback**. Useful location details: building/storefront type, floor/access restrictions, parking, gates, operating hours, contact/manager, interior access permission, pets, fragile items, water access, and customer-preferred service window. A building/property is a durable record; window/pane count and serviceable surface details can be captured as job/quote inputs or a configurable site asset if reused. Window anchor/rope access, ladder constraints, access equipment, and required skill/training are safety-sensitive. Customer-visible notes and technician-only security/access instructions should be separated. Do not treat each pane as a persistent CRM asset by default.

## Services, intake, quoting, and booking

Common work includes exterior/interior glass, screens, tracks/sills, storefront rounds, post-construction cleanup, hard-water/stain removal, and sometimes gutters or solar panels. These are service-specific add-ons, not universal. Intake should ask property type, approximate pane/window count, floors, interior/exterior, screens/tracks, access method, condition, photos, and preferred time. A forum-hosted quote worksheet recommends collecting window count and access details then verifying with a property walk-around. Residential work can be per-window or estimated from photos/site visit; commercial bids may specify scope, frequency, and service window. The choice to quote instantly or require review depends on access, height, and complexity. Customer self-service can request a quote with photos; high-rise or unusual access should be staff-reviewed.

## Pricing and commercial model

Evidence-backed quote variables include surface/pane count, inside/outside, screens/tracks, floor/access method, soil/stain/paint condition, ladder/pole/lift/rope work, job location, repeat frequency, and commercial service window. The operator worksheet and community posts show that access and scope complexity affect quotes; there is no single pricing method established across businesses. One-time jobs may use deposits or cancellation terms; recurring routes may invoice per clean or on a regular billing cycle, but this is tenant policy. **Software willingness-to-pay:** Squeegee's UK page, accessed 2026-09-24, advertises plans starting at £15.83/month plus VAT and per-user charging; the interactive comparison did not expose static tier prices. This is current vendor list-price evidence, not proof of US willingness-to-pay. A Customer Factor page has historic stale price claims and was not used for current price conclusions. No software budget should be inferred from cleaning service prices.

## Recurrence, scheduling, dispatch, and routing

Residential rounds can repeat at a customer-defined interval; storefront accounts may be monthly or set to a recurring route day. Forum operators describe preferred time windows, restaurant busy periods, and a route growing by neighboring businesses. Weather interruption varies by region, customer preference, and service scope: some operators work in rain or complete interiors; others reschedule entire jobs or offer a limited touch-up guarantee. Support exact appointments for customer-present interior work, flexible windows for exteriors, recurring rounds, route days, commercial blackout windows, weather delays, partial completion, return visits, and customer reschedule/cancel requests. A 2014 Canada thread shows that rescheduling every customer after rain can create territory-wide route cascades. Route optimization should allow human constraints and manual override.

## Field workflow, safety, completion, and rework

Suggested flow: review property/service notes and work-at-height plan; confirm access and service scope; protect interiors/property; clean specified surfaces using approved equipment; note inaccessible/damaged glass or extra soil; record completed areas and any partial work; capture optional photos/customer sign-off; invoice or return to finish. OSHA 29 CFR 1910.140 sets personal fall-protection criteria including specific window-cleaner positioning-system provisions. IWCA identifies separate route/residential/light-commercial and rope-descent training. Actual method depends on work height, equipment, building anchorage, and applicable standards; the CRM should record applicable training/equipment checks rather than prescribe a universal method. Noncompletion candidates: locked/no interior access, weather, unsafe height/access, broken/damaged glass, customer cancellation, equipment issue, or partial interior-only visit. Callback/touch-up terms vary by company and contract.

## Customer communication and self-service

Customers need quote confirmation, access instructions, appointment/reminder, arrival/delay, weather policy, partial-completion explanation, invoice/payment, and recurring visit reminders. Portal/form functions can request a quote, share photos, set interior access preference, view upcoming clean, pay, request changes, and report missed spots. Avoid automatic promises about rain guarantees, free touch-ups, glass damage responsibility, or reschedule timing; these are company policy. For commercial sites, messages should coordinate with site contact and avoid customer business peak hours.

## Equipment, inventory, suppliers, and workforce

Squeegees, applicators, water-fed poles, ladders, screens tools, hoses, purified-water systems, lifts, rope-access gear, PPE, and vehicles may be relevant. Equipment requirements change sharply with service scope. Inventory tracking and maintenance reminders are optional. Route/window cleaning uses different skills from rope-descent/high-rise teams; the Squeegee product exposes required skills and team assignment, while IWCA offers safety training. Time and payroll inputs depend on staffing model. Do not assume all operators use lifts, carry customer-specific supplies, or hire employees.

## Reporting and operating measures

Candidate measures: completed cleans by route/property, revenue per stop or work hour, route density/travel, recurring-customer retention, quote conversion, rework/touch-up rate, weather-caused deferral, crew utilization, and commercial account profitability. These are inferred operational measures; the sources do not establish a common KPI vocabulary. Larger companies may need training status and site-level safety records. Separate service output from safety compliance reporting.

## Existing software and operator evidence

Squeegee is purpose-built around window-cleaning rounds and markets map-based jobs, property notes, scheduling, quoting, customer notices, recurring accounts, and payments. The Customer Factor also markets window cleaning workflows, but its public pricing copy is stale. General field platforms such as Jobber also appear in operator discussions. First-person Window Cleaning Resource contributors describe manual scheduling, growing local routes, customer time windows, weather disruption, and differing rework policies. A UK forum operator reported route interval/date bunching; treat this as a software edge case rather than market-wide defect. Product prices are UK pounds, and geography limits their comparison to US vendor prices. The accessible Squeegee page shows a starting price but dynamic plan controls obscure the full pricing table. No representative operator-reported CRM purchase price was found.

## Evidence, disagreements, and uncertainty

Sources agree on recurring rounds, site notes, route planning, and need for access awareness. They disagree sharply on rain response, weather guarantees, and the balance between flexible windows and exact appointments. OSHA requirements apply to employers/work at height as specified by the standard; IWCA training is trade guidance. UK pricing/product terminology should not be generalized to US economics. Confidence is **high** for route-and-property workflow and safety variation, **medium** for common quote inputs, and **low** for prevalence of high-rise work, common KPI definitions, or exact software budget. Unanswered: how often solo cleaners manage entirely by paper/calendar; regional seasonality; current plan prices in other markets; and precise customer/insurance documentation needs for high-rise work.

## Handoff to a future pack implementer

Prototype route/round scheduling with property-specific access notes, quote scope for surface count and access, recurring service, customer weather/reschedule preferences, partial-completion and touch-up handling. Ask service height/method, residential/commercial mix, route cadence, interior access requirements, recurring billing policy, crew skills, and weather guarantee. Keep safety documentation configurable and visible to the right staff. Test with both solo storefront routes and team-based high-access work before setting defaults. Keep service prices and callbacks tenant-defined.
