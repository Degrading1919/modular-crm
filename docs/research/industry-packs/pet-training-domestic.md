## Executive summary

**Observed evidence:** Domestic dog training is a service and coaching relationship: an owner describes goals and the dog/household context; a trainer assesses fit, delivers an evaluation and lessons/program, records progress, and gives owners practice/homework between sessions. Delivery can be private, in-home, at a training location, group classes, virtual coaching, or board-and-train. A first-person independent trainer describes an initial consult, living-situation and behavior intake, fit/referral decision, estimate, session packages, follow-up email/resources, and homework. Training software vendors emphasize dog-centered records, notes, packages, class/session schedules, forms/waivers, progress summaries, and owner access.

**Inference:** A useful pack should focus on owner/household/dog plus goals, behavior history, training plan, session notes, measurable progress, and client homework; do not inherit pet-waste recurring cleanup or pet-sitting visit assumptions. Group classes and board-and-train require conditional capacity and care workflows; aggressive/reactive or medical cases require trainer review and potentially referral. No universal dog-trainer license, credential, training method, vaccination rule, or legal intake schema is established by this research. Standards apply only within their stated membership/certification scope.

## Business model and customer segments

Independent trainers, small teams, training schools, pet facilities, and franchise programs sell an initial assessment/consultation, private lessons, multi-session packages, group classes, behavior consulting, virtual instruction, and—in some businesses—board-and-train. Customers are pet owners/caretakers seeking practical skills or help with behavior concerns. Referral channels can include veterinary practices, shelters/rescues, breeders, online search/social profiles, and repeat clients; this profile does not estimate channel shares. A case involving safety concerns, medical questions, or needs beyond trainer competence may be referred to a veterinarian or qualified behavior professional; one first-person trainer explicitly described disclosing limits and refunding/referring when not the best fit.

Most recurring contact is a sequence or package rather than an indefinite recurring service route. Session frequency and program length depend on goals, owner practice, dog response, trainer model, and class schedule. Solo trainers often work at client homes or public locations and manage sales/admin themselves; facility trainers have class calendars and room/dog capacity; board-and-train adds temporary custody, daily care, safety, staff handoffs, and owner updates. Do not assume board-and-train is universal merely because it appears in some software offerings.

## Terminology and durable records

Use **client/guardian**, **household**, **dog/pet**, **training case**, **assessment/evaluation**, **lesson/session**, **program/package**, **homework/practice plan**, and **trainer**. The durable asset is the animal and its training/behavior history, connected to one or more guardians and household members. Useful fields include name/photo, age/breed/size, household members, goals, relevant medical/behavior history, triggers/context, bite or injury history where disclosed and relevant, current management/safety plan, skills/behaviors worked, response/progress, accommodations, and veterinary/other-professional contacts when provided. This information can be sensitive: collect what is relevant, restrict staff access, obtain required client permissions before sharing case data/photos, and avoid presenting a trainer’s notes as a diagnosis.

Service location is conditional: client home, trainer facility, park/public site, virtual, or boarding facility. In-home assessment may need household layout, yard/fence, access, other animals/people, and environmental triggers. Group classes need class location, room/participant capacity, entry/exit and dog separation arrangements set by the operator. Equipment used in training (leash, harness, reward tools, barriers) is generally the operator’s or client’s equipment, not a persistent CRM customer asset; record relevant equipment/instructions on the dog/case or session only when useful. Training methods/tool policy must be explicit tenant policy, not inferred from a generic field.

## Services, intake, quoting, and booking

Menu candidates include phone discovery call, in-person or remote evaluation, private lesson, multi-session package, group class/course, puppy foundations, manners/skills, behavior consultation, follow-up, day training, and board-and-train. Species, age, risk, trainer qualification/scope, facility, and training philosophy change the offering. Intake should capture the client’s stated goals and expectations, dog and household details, behavior history/context, relevant health information, prior training, household members/other animals, safety concerns, location, availability, preferred format, and referral/consent needs. A trainer may review intake before booking to determine fit, urgency, safe location, or referral. Do not automatically sort clients into a diagnosis, risk grade, or guaranteed outcome.

Simple first calls may be free or paid; private consultations may be individually priced; programs/classes often use a session package or enrollment fee. One owner/operator reports an initial $70 foundation lesson and packages at $189 for three and $280 for five sessions in southwest Michigan, posted in a 2022 discussion (prices are historical, local, and not current market benchmarks). Package/session credits need tracking and clear rules for expiration, missed sessions, cancellations, refunds, and transfers. Group classes require published dates, capacity, prerequisites, waitlist, and enrollment confirmation. Board-and-train requires explicit dates and facility capacity plus care agreement, intake, and handoff; its booking is not merely a series of lesson appointments. Online booking should support request/approval or discovery call before confirming higher-risk or complex cases.

## Pricing and commercial model

Evidence supports different commercial shapes: assessment/consultation, per-session pricing, multi-session packages, group-class enrollment, and board-and-train programs. Inputs can include session length, trainer/credential/experience, travel/location, case complexity, course size, package discount, boarding days, and included follow-up; specific formulas vary. Obtain informed agreement on scope, payment, cancellation, refund/transfer, and outcome expectations. A trainer explicitly cautions that dog outcomes cannot be guaranteed and describes matching work to their area of competence.

**Software willingness-to-pay evidence:** Pupline’s vendor page lists Pro at $12.99/month and Facilities at $49.98/month for three staff plus $6 per extra seat; it contrasts these with a claimed Gingr starting price of about $114/location and about $200 setup, which the seller dates to May 2026. These are public vendor list-price/competitive claims accessed 2026-09-24, not independent purchases. Other software pages offer custom/demo pricing. Operator posts mention price, setup burden, complexity, and desire not to combine apps, but do not document a verified purchase price. Do not infer CRM budget from lesson/program prices.

## Recurrence, scheduling, dispatch, and routing

Schedule one-time consultations, individual lessons, recurring standing weekly sessions, multi-week programs, group classes, virtual calls, and date-range board-and-train. Private lessons use appointment times and travel buffers for in-home work; programs should connect sessions to a case/package and show remaining credits. Groups need enrollment/capacity and trainer/facility availability. Owner homework and practice occur between scheduled jobs and should not be modeled as extra technician dispatches. Board-and-train reserves animal/premises capacity across dates, with daily care assignments and staff handoffs. Schedule changes, no-shows, makeups, cancellations, and instructor illness need human/tenant rules. Route optimization is optional for trainers traveling to multiple home clients, and usually irrelevant to a facility or virtual-only trainer.

## Field workflow, safety, completion, and rework

Suggested lifecycle: client inquiry; screen for fit and urgent safety/health concerns; gather dog/household context; agree on service, consent, scope, fees and location; conduct assessment; record observable baseline and proposed goals; deliver lesson/program; log what was practiced and the dog’s response; send owner recap/homework/resources; schedule follow-up; review progress and adapt or refer. Do not present training services as veterinary diagnosis or promise behavior outcomes. For group or public sessions, record the trainer’s tenant-defined safety plan and attendance. For board-and-train, add arrival/condition, feeding/care instructions, daily training/care notes, incident escalation, owner updates, and return handoff.

Professional standards are not uniform law: CCPDT’s code binds its certificants and says its scope includes safe, humane practice, confidentiality, use of standards, and enforcement. IAABC states its members must refrain from intentional shock use; its joint standards are endorsed by named organizations. These are standards of professional organizations, not evidence of universal jurisdictional rules. The specific training approach should be visible in the trainer’s own policies and agreed with the client. Record incidents, bite/injury, safety-plan changes, referral, and informed consent with access controls. Do not automatically send advice or alter a safety plan based on AI/automation. Noncompletion may include client/dog no-show, unsafe location, illness, dog distress, client not prepared, trainer scope/competence limit, severe weather, or facility capacity; referral, rescheduling, refund, or early termination are tenant decisions.

## Customer communication and self-service

Useful communication includes inquiry response, screening/evaluation instructions, agreement/waiver, payment/package status, reminder, session recap, homework/resources, progress review, class notices, cancellation/makeup, and board-and-train updates. Client portals or secure private links may share forms, dog details, session notes, media, invoices, and exercises. One training-first vendor allows owners to open recaps from a private link without creating an account; another operator describes email as the written record and sends resources/homework after sessions. Customers may self-submit goals/history, book an approved session, complete waivers/intake, pay, review their plan, and request follow-up. Trainers should review sensitive behavior/medical information and confirm consent before external sharing or media use. Automated reminders are low risk; automated diagnostic claims, safety instructions, or assurances are not.

## Equipment, inventory, suppliers, and workforce

Tools vary with method, species, venue, class, and case: treats/rewards, leashes/harnesses, long lines, barriers, mats, targets, clickers, cleaning supplies, and facility equipment. No universal tracked inventory system is evidenced. Staffed facilities may need instructor assignment, class enrollment, room capacity, time/payroll, and coverage. Independent trainers often handle scheduling, client communication, marketing, and lesson preparation themselves. For board-and-train, boarding equipment, feeding supplies, sanitation, and shift handoff may be essential. Do not impose a particular method’s equipment or inventory assumptions as pack defaults.

## Reporting and operating measures

Candidate measures: inquiry-to-evaluation conversion, evaluation-to-program conversion, session/package utilization, attendance/no-show/cancellation, package expiration/credits, revenue by service/trainer/referral source, class enrollment and fill rate, training goals/progress checkpoints, follow-up completion, referral/out-of-scope outcomes, client satisfaction, and (if board-and-train) occupancy and incidents. Progress measures are case-specific and must be trainer-defined rather than scored through a universal behavior metric. These KPIs are mostly inferred; software sellers advertise progress tracking and revenue reporting, but broad adoption is not established.

## Existing software and operator evidence

Training-first software markets session notes, dog profiles, homework/progress, packages, forms/waivers, scheduling, invoices, and portals. Broader pet platforms such as Gingr position facility plans around classes/training alongside grooming/boarding. Trainers and prospective customers also mention BusyPaws, PetPocketBook, Time To Pet, PocketSuite, Acuity, Square, Google/Outlook Calendar, Excel, Notion, and email. A first-person Michigan trainer described an intake-to-consult-to-package workflow and using email, homework, and referrals; a small part-time operator described Outlook/Excel and a written email trail. A Canadian pet professional seeking a CRM alternative said feature overload added mental load and requested better calendar sync, date-span overnight booking, rescheduling, packages, intake-to-profile flow, secure waivers/documents, and intuitive setup; they explicitly wanted to avoid combining tools and already used Notion for training notes. This represents a multi-service pet-care business, not a pure trainer. A training software vendor says its product was built after a founder/trainer found existing software did not fit, but this is vendor-originated evidence and not an independent operator survey. Common friction signals include scattered records, admin overhead, package tracking, progress communication, feature overload/setup effort, and mismatch between facility versus solo trainer pricing/workflow.

## Evidence, disagreements, and uncertainty

Professional bodies agree on safety, competence limits, confidentiality, and responsible treatment, but their standards have different membership scopes and do not establish a universal legal standard. Business models vary from solo lessons to classes and facility stays; software that is a fit for a board-and-train operation can feel excessive to a private trainer. Operator evidence supports practical needs for intake, evaluation, package billing, email records, and homework, but software-pain evidence is less mature and is partly vendor-authored. Important unanswered questions: how often domestic pet trainers handle non-dog species; what behavior information they consider safe/necessary to store; how group classes document dog/handler compatibility; and whether the shared scheduler supports class capacity, package credits, and date-span occupancy. Confidence is **high** for scheduling and pet/client records, **medium** for structured homework/progress defaults and packages, and **low** for compliance defaults, typical software budget, or outcome KPIs. Reddit is anonymous and selected; CCPDT/IAABC guidance applies to their members; commercial vendor pages describe vendor positioning.

## Handoff to a future pack implementer

Prototype a private-lesson workflow with inquiry/screening, owner and dog profile, household/context intake, assessment, chosen service/package, consent, lesson schedule, trainer notes, client-facing recap/homework, payment, and follow-up. Ask whether the business offers group classes, virtual coaching, home visits, behavior cases, or board-and-train; what training approaches/credentials it uses; how it handles referrals and safety concerns; and how packages and missed sessions work. Keep method, case-specific goals, outcome measures, risk notes, waiver, price, package policy, and scope/referral rules tenant-controlled. Confirm shared support for package credits, class capacity, private client/pet notes, and board-and-train date-range occupancy before treating those as platform gaps.
