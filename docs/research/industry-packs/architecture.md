## Executive summary

Architecture firms sell design and professional services to residential owners, developers, businesses, institutions, contractors, and public clients. Small remodels and custom homes differ from commercial, institutional, and large multi-consultant projects. AIA's B101 standard agreement organizes common services into schematic design, design development, construction documents, procurement, and construction administration; those are service phases, not a universal mandatory sequence. Core artifacts include briefs, options, BIM/CAD models, drawings/specifications, consultant coordination, permit sets, bid addenda, RFIs/submittals, site reports, and closeout information.

**Observed evidence:** NCARB points candidates to state licensing boards; AIA contract resources distinguish project sizes and scopes. Small-firm practitioners describe generic PM tools as weak for fee forecasting and architecture-specific financial controls, while AIA reports low profitability as a management challenge for smaller firms. **Inference:** phase fee burn, staffing capacity, proposals, decisions, scope/change and client approvals are useful CRM/project-operations functions. CAD/BIM, code review, sealed engineering, and construction delivery remain specialist tools and professional responsibilities. No architecture-only core gap is established.

## Business model and customer segments

Customers and decision-makers include homeowners, property developers, business owners, real estate operators, contractors, institutional/facility owners, and public agencies. Work includes feasibility/programming, new buildings, tenant fit-out, renovations/additions, custom homes, interiors, master planning, existing-building documentation, and construction-phase support. Practices specialize: residential architects may work directly with owners and builders; commercial practices often work for developers or institutions with consultants and formal procurement; some architects provide visualization, interiors, planning, or limited-scope design.

Triggers include purchase/development, lease or tenant change, expansion, code/permit needs, accessibility, renovation, damage, and capital planning. Referral, repeat owner, contractor, developer relationship, selection process, and web inquiry can all lead work. Small studios may have one principal handling sales, design, and invoicing; larger firms have sector leaders, PMs, technical staff, BIM managers, and marketing/admin. Not every architect contracts for or administers construction; task scope must say what services are included.

## Terminology and durable records

Use owner/client, project, site/building, program, phase, consultant, design option, drawing/model/specification set, issue/revision, permit, bid/procurement, addendum, RFI, submittal, site observation, punch list, and closeout. Store owner/entity and authorized contacts, site/parcels, jurisdiction, occupancy/use, area/program, existing conditions and source, budget target, schedule, accessibility/code criteria references, survey/geotechnical background, consultant team, builder, and permission/access requirements. A building can be a persistent asset for repeat renovations, but design projects are separate records with separate contracts and revisions.

Maintain decisions, client approvals, consultant backgrounds, drawing/model revision IDs, transmittals, meeting minutes, scope exclusions, addenda, field observations and limitations, and superseded issue history. Distinguish client approval of a concept from permit approval, engineering sign-off, or construction completion. Plans and sensitive property/security data should be restricted by project role; retain who received which version.

## Services, intake, quoting, and booking

Intake should ask project/site and jurisdiction, owner authority, building use/type/area, new vs existing, objectives, budget and timing, available surveys/plans/photos, permitting status, contractor or consultants engaged, requested services/phases, and decision-makers. Concept meetings/site visits are scheduled engagements, not automated instant service appointments. Potential clients need fit/availability review before a proposal.

Proposal/contract commonly defines basic services and phases, deliverables, client responsibilities, consultants, additional services, assumptions, reimbursables, budget role, schedule, and construction-phase scope. AIA publishes multiple agreement forms (e.g., B105 for small projects and B103 for large/complex work), indicating scope/contract varies with project size. Fee can be lump sum, hourly, percentage or hybrid depending on scope/client; use editable fee and phase assumptions. Revision/approval gates, owner-directed changes, consultant input and permit comments drive schedule and fee. Online inquiry/file upload can aid intake; no generic quote or booking should promise project fit.

## Pricing and commercial model

Fee inputs include project type/size, complexity, phase coverage, consultant coordination, existing conditions, code/permit effort, schedule/urgency, deliverable level, reimbursables, and construction administration. Fees may be phase-based lump sum, hourly, percentage of construction cost, or negotiated combinations; actual choices vary by market and contract. AIA advises documenting the architect's obligation around client budget and treatment when bids exceed budget; budgets and construction cost are not guaranteed estimates unless contractually defined.

Track authorized base services, earned fee, phase budget vs actual hours, invoiced/paid, consultant pass-throughs, additional services/change authorization, and forecast to complete. Billing may be periodic against phase progress or time; late owner decisions, unpaid invoices, bid overages, and unpaid redesign can threaten small-firm cash flow. Forum posts mention fee tracking and forecasting friction but do not support a reliable CRM WTP figure. Revit/BIM license pricing is a separate production expense, not evidence of software budget for CRM.

## Recurrence, scheduling, dispatch, and routing

Projects move through briefing, concept, design development, documentation, permit review, procurement, and construction administration, with iteration and overlapping phases common. Dependencies include owner decisions, survey and consultant backgrounds, planning/zoning hearings, permitting, contractor pricing, long-lead decisions, submittals, and construction access. Staff allocation depends on design skill, project phase and deadlines; firms manage project fee and individual workload concurrently. Field visits occur at milestones or on request; construction observation is not continuous supervision unless contract says so. Route optimization is secondary. Document project holds, approvals, and critical dependencies.

## Field workflow, safety, completion, and rework

Field workflows include existing-condition measurement/documentation and scheduled construction observations. Record project/visit scope, date/weather if relevant, areas visible/inaccessible, progress observed, photos, questions/deficiencies, contractor/client notification, and follow-up. AIA construction-phase services are contractual; an observation should not be recorded as a guarantee of contractor performance or full-time inspection. Revisions can arise from client decisions, budget, permit comments, coordination clashes, contractor substitutions, or field variance. Track requestor, decision, affected sheets/model/spec, impacts, fee, approval, and reissued documents.

Architect licensure and title are jurisdictional; architects coordinate with separately licensed structural/civil/MEP professionals as project needs require. Preserve authored/checked/issued roles and document versions, and do not use CRM approvals as professional seals or permit approval. Noncompletion reasons can include missing owner decisions, funding, access, consultant input, permit hold, contractor delay, and scope disagreement. Keep client signoff distinct from AHJ acceptance and contractor completion.

## Customer communication and self-service

Clients need clear proposal/scope, design review meetings, options and decision requests, milestone status, permit/review notices, current drawings, bid clarifications, site visit reports, change approvals, invoice and closeout. Controlled document portals and email are useful, but should identify current issue and authorized recipients. Customer-facing views may show released deliverables and decisions; draft design notes/internal coordination should remain internal. Automated reminders can chase decisions and invoices; technical advice, commitments, bid-budget implications and scope amendments need human review.

## Equipment, inventory, suppliers, and workforce

Typical tool stack includes Revit/ArchiCAD or CAD, BIM coordination/model viewers, PDF markup, rendering, spreadsheets, specifications, file storage, email/calendar, time capture, and accounting/practice-management. Consultants include civil, structural, MEP, landscape, interiors, code, cost, survey, and specialty design; consultants are contracted/managed according to owner agreement and scope. Staff roles include principal, project architect/PM, designer, technical/BIM staff, intern, and admin/marketing. Capture role, project assignment, capacity, billable time, and jurisdictional license where needed. Physical materials belong to contractor/supplier workflow, not a default architecture inventory.

## Reporting and operating measures

AIA/Deltek Architecture Billings Index surveys firm billings; small-firm guidance notes profitability/management challenges. Useful practice measures include signed backlog, weighted pipeline, proposal win rate, earned/billed fee by phase, hours and forecast to complete, effective hourly realization, utilization, consultant costs, receivables aging, scope amendment time, decision/permit cycle time, design revision count, and construction-phase response time. Measures differ by firm size and delivery model; distinguish observed industry metrics from inferred CRM dashboards.

## Existing software and operator evidence

Firms commonly combine BIM/CAD (Autodesk Revit is one incumbent), cloud model coordination, drawing/specification management, PDF review, email/calendar, task tracking, timesheets, invoicing/accounting, and spreadsheet fee forecasts. Monograph specifically markets to architecture and engineering practices and its support documents QuickBooks Online syncing, projects, timesheets and invoice workflows. Deltek Ajera is another AEC practice/financial system; a small-firm practitioner thread reports timesheets work adequately but billing is painful (anecdotal, one user's experience). Generic Asana-style tools can help tasks but a small-firm operator describes weak fee tracking/forecasting; other firms report workable alternatives and different firm sizes use different stacks.

Vendor pages do not give a stable universal price in the sources reviewed; some sales require current quote or account plan. Subscription and seat pricing should be checked at purchase. Operator complaints describe workflow friction, not willingness to pay a new vendor. No actual price paid for a comparable CRM was verified; do not use billing fees or BIM seats to infer CRM WTP.

## Evidence, disagreements, and uncertainty

AIA's phase/contract framework is a useful US reference, but smaller practices combine phases and project delivery methods vary. Public, institutional, residential, commercial, and interiors work have different sales and fee models. NCARB links licensing to jurisdiction boards; validate each state. Operator reports differ on which generic or vertical tools work, often by firm size and accounting needs. Confidence is high for phased service and jurisdictional licensing, medium for small-firm fee-management friction and common tool categories, and low for current competitor price and software WTP. Validate exact package price, migration demand, and construction document portal expectations.

## Handoff to a future pack implementer

Prototype phase-aware project intake, decisions and approvals, fee burn/forecast, deliverable issue/revision log, consultant coordination, and construction-visit records. Ask project segment, contract/fee model, phase coverage, jurisdictions, accounting and BIM/document stack, and who may approve released files. Keep firm-defined phases, contract clauses, and consultant rules configurable. Confirm an auditable release/version model and current competitor price before treating either as a product gap or pricing anchor.
