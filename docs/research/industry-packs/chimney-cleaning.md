## Executive summary

**Observed evidence:** Chimney-sweep businesses may combine cleaning/sweeping, condition inspection, written/photo report, and separately estimated repairs such as caps, liners, masonry, or relining. CSIA material describes three inspection levels with different scopes and triggers; the inspection is a professional evaluation, not just a cleaning checklist. Operators face seasonal booking surges and annual follow-up, while small firms may still use paper reports. Purpose-built software vendors market property/chimney history, inspection forms/photos, annual reminders, customer reports, estimates, and repair follow-up.

**Inference:** The durable customer asset is the chimney/flue/appliance system at a property, because inspection and repair history informs later visits. A pack should distinguish a routine sweep/Level I workflow from Level II/III inspections, diagnostics, and repair projects, without making an inspection level or annual interval an automatic legal mandate. This sector has a stronger case than routine exterior cleaning for structured inspection findings and linked repair follow-up; the reviewed evidence does not prove that a reusable shared forms/report capability is inadequate, so there is no confirmed core gap.

## Business model and customer segments

Residential homeowners with fireplaces, wood/pellet stoves, or other vented appliances book cleaning and safety inspections. Other buyers can include landlords, property managers, real-estate parties, insurers, builders, and commercial facilities with solid-fuel appliances. Most routine work is property-specific; a shop may dispatch multiple technicians during the fall/winter demand peak and perform repair or installation work in off-peak windows. Purchase triggers include seasonal appliance use, smoke/draft/odor issues, move-in, an appliance/fuel change, visible damage, or an inspection recommendation. Annual recontact is marketed by incumbents and supported by customer reports of scheduling next year's service, but exact interval and local code obligations vary. Solo sweeps need a calendar/customer history; teams add capacity, route and skill assignment, and report review. Larger masonry/reline projects require estimates, materials, permits, multiple visits, and a distinct workflow from a one-hour sweep.

## Terminology and durable records

Use **customer/homeowner**, **property/site**, **chimney**, **flue/liner**, **connected appliance/fireplace**, **sweep/cleaning**, **inspection level**, **finding/deficiency**, **recommendation**, **estimate**, **repair/reline**, and **technician**. Property/site data includes address, access, occupancy/contact, appliance/fuel type, roof access, parking, pets, and prior service date. A chimney/flue and connected appliance are durable assets; fields may include type/material, liner, location, served appliance/fuel, cap/crown/damper/firebox components, known condition, photos/video scans, inspection level/date, obstruction/combustible deposits, measurements, recommendations, and repair history. Treat Level 1/2/3 findings and supporting media as professional service records; restrict internal notes appropriately and present signed customer reports deliberately. Carbon-monoxide/fire risk, roof access, and suspected unsafe conditions are safety-critical. Distinguish a finding from a diagnosis, code determination, or guarantee. Fuel use, creosote/debris, and current blockage are visit-specific findings.

## Services, intake, quoting, and booking

Service menu candidates: routine chimney/flue sweep; Level I inspection; Level II inspection when conditions warrant; Level III investigative inspection when concealed areas require access; fireplace/stove/vent inspection; camera scan; cap/crown/damper/liner/masonry repairs; relining; animal/debris removal; and appliance installation/alteration review. These branches require trained judgment and, for repairs, qualifications/permits by locality. CSIA explains that Level I generally covers readily accessible portions in routine maintenance with unchanged conditions; Level II expands accessible areas and may include video scanning for changed conditions; Level III is invasive access where needed to investigate concealed portions. Intake asks property and contact, appliance/fuel, last service/known issues, requested cleaning/inspection, appliance/fuel change, sale/event trigger, roof/access constraints, symptoms, and photos/documents. Triage should never diagnose remotely. Estimate cleaning/inspection as a defined scope; inspection findings may generate a separately approved repair estimate or multi-visit project. Calendar booking may be online for routine work with office review for no-access/roof/chimney type uncertainty, smoke/CO reports, or invasive inspections.

## Pricing and commercial model

Pricing inputs include number/type of chimneys/flues/appliances, inspection level, access/roof pitch/height, camera scan, soot/creosote/debris condition, system construction, travel, cleanup/protection, repair materials/labor, permits, and emergency/after-hours response. Customer forum accounts from two decades ago report flat-rate examples and multi-flue discounts; they are old and geography-specific, not current benchmark prices. A 2024 anonymous operator post describes local pricing and demand but cannot establish typical economics. Routine services may be due on a recurring reminder; maintenance plans, per-visit payments, deposits for larger repairs, progress billing, financing, warranties, and cancellations are tenant/company policy. **Software willingness-to-pay:** Jobkeepr's chimney-focused page currently advertises $39/month for one user plus $10/month per additional user. This is an incumbent list price, not verified operator spending. Other vendor content advertises prices, but some pages are co-founder/vendor-authored and not independent. Do not infer software budget from chimney service prices.

## Recurrence, scheduling, dispatch, and routing

Annual reminders and seasonal booking are strongly present in chimney-specific software marketing and customer/operator accounts, but actual service frequency follows appliance use, findings, manufacturer guidance, contracts, and applicable code. Fall rushes can be followed by quieter months; a company may schedule repairs and inspections outside peak sweep season. Support due-date/follow-up reminders separate from guaranteed appointments, capacity controls, appointment windows, travel-aware territory routes, multi-chimney/multi-property visits, seasonal surge waitlists, return visits, repair dependencies, and emergency/hazard escalations. Assign technicians by scope, training, equipment, and inspection/report authority where the business requires it. A reported unsafe condition may require stopping appliance use and prompt professional handling; automated messages must avoid interpreting findings or prescribing a remedy.

## Field workflow, safety, completion, and rework

Suggested lifecycle: confirm appliance/chimney identity and service history; clarify customer-reported symptoms and scope; protect the work area and use appropriate PPE; inspect relevant accessible system/appliance components at the selected level; determine whether cleaning is appropriate and perform agreed sweeping; capture findings and required measurements/photos/video; classify condition and recommendations; review urgent hazard/escalation with customer; generate a report signed/approved by the responsible professional; present separate repair options; obtain approval before additional work; close out invoice and follow-up reminders. CSIA’s Level I guidance calls out routine cleaning and a 1/8-inch combustible-deposit sweep threshold; verify current applicable standard and local rules before using as an automated checklist. NFPA 211 inspection-level terms are code-referenced; applicability depends on adopted code, system, and professional judgment. Licensure/certification regimes vary; CSIA certification is a credential, not a universal statutory requirement. Noncompletion reasons: no access/roof unsafe, appliance not available/cool per policy, animal obstruction, unsafe condition requiring stop, equipment/camera failure, scope upgrade needed, customer deferral, severe weather, or incomplete multi-visit repair. Distinguish no-clean inspection, inspection finding, repair revisit, warranty callback, and customer dissatisfaction.

## Customer communication and self-service

Customers need booking/arrival reminders, preparation/access instructions, inspection scope/level explanation, report with photos and plain-language findings, urgent safety escalation, separately priced options, invoice/payment, and next due-date reminders. Portal/self-service can request routine service, supply appliance/chimney details, view/download signed reports and photos, approve estimates, pay, schedule repair, and update contacts. Do not auto-generate definitive safety conclusions, code compliance claims, or recommendations without technician review. Urgent findings and changes in scope need direct human communication; reminders should say service is due/recommended under the shop's policy rather than imply a universal legal requirement.

## Equipment, inventory, suppliers, and workforce

Brushes/rods, vacuums/HEPA filtration, drop cloths, inspection cameras, ladders/roof safety equipment, PPE, replacement caps/liners/masonry materials, and service vehicles can be relevant. Repair/install crews need material procurement and multi-visit coordination beyond sweep inventory. Equipment and credential tracking are conditional on shop size and level of service. Technicians may need distinct inspection, camera, repair, and report-review skills; the accountable professional must sign/approve the report. Time capture, mileage, payroll, and subcontractors vary. The available sources do not establish common piece-rate or commission practices.

## Reporting and operating measures

Potential measures: inspections/sweeps by property and due date, seasonal capacity/lead time, reminder-to-book conversion, report completion/review time, findings by component/severity, repair estimates approved, repair backlog, return visits, callback rate, technician utilization, and revenue by cleaning/repair. Incumbents market report and seasonal rebooking features; only some are direct vendor claims, and KPI definitions are not standardized. Safety findings and regulatory documentation should be auditable separately from sales measures.

## Existing software and operator evidence

Chimney-focused products such as Jobkeepr, LayCor, Bella FSM, and FieldChimney market some combination of annual reminders, chimney/property history, inspection levels/reports, photos, repair estimates, dispatch, invoices, and payments. Vendor feature claims are not proof of adoption or efficacy. Jobkeepr publishes $39/month for one user, with added-user pricing. A 2025 Reddit feature discussion includes a small chimney business owner saying they still use paper reports and arguing for owner-configurable inspection templates; this is a useful but single anonymous voice. A homeowner discussion describes flat-rate cleaning/inspection and booking the following year before departure, but dates from 2006. A 2024 first-person business-owner story describes customer price objections and local demand; it is anecdotal and not a software complaint. No representative survey of software stacks, migration concerns, or operator willingness to pay was found. Large enterprise software comparisons authored by vendor founders were excluded from prevalence claims.

## Evidence, disagreements, and uncertainty

The strongest agreement is that inspection scope varies by level and chimney/appliance history matters. Software vendors converge on annual reminders, reports/photos, and repair estimates; that concurrence shows product positioning but not operator adoption. Customer and operator discussions are anecdotal and sometimes old. Code adoption and credentials differ by jurisdiction, fuel, appliance, and job; CSIA is a trade body and its certification is not itself a universal license. Confidence is **high** for distinct inspection scope and persistent system history, **medium** for seasonal recurrence and repair conversion, and **low** for software market share, common pricing, and prevalence of paper versus digital reports. Open questions: whether technicians need customer report signatures, required retention periods by state/insurer, applicable current NFPA edition and local adoption, and whether current shared CRM reports support post-issuance correction history.

## Handoff to a future pack implementer

Prototype the chimney/flue/appliance as a history-bearing site asset, routine cleaning and inspection workflows with distinct levels, technician-reviewed report/media, due-date reminders, and findings-to-estimate follow-up. Ask appliance/fuel types, inspection/repair scope, inspection credentials and local requirements, digital report/signature needs, season and peak capacity, and crew/report approval roles. Keep recurrence, report language, service price, access method, credential rules, and warranty policy tenant-configurable. Before treating report provenance as a core gap, confirm whether current shared forms/media already provide controlled issuance and revision history.
