## Executive summary

**Observed evidence:** This profile interprets “cistern / water gatherer” as rainwater catchment and storage installation: assess roof/catchment and demand, size/location a tank, prepare pad or excavation, route gutters/downspouts, screen/filter/first-flush, manage overflow, connect pump/pressure and distribution, then test, commission and explain maintenance. Systems may be irrigation-only, other non-potable uses, or potable supply; those are not interchangeable. Potable systems need much more stringent source/treatment/cross-connection, inspection and ongoing water-quality controls, defined by applicable code and authority.

The durable customer record is a cistern + treatment/pump asset at a property, with intended use, capacity, model/serial, plumbing/electrical details, service/inspection history and maintenance. Pack opportunity: site assessment and project records with a human-reviewed use/permit checklist, commissioning and service schedule. Evidence for technical workflow is strong, including U.S. operator examples and official standards/regulatory guidance; direct CRM software use and WTP are undocumented. Status remains partial because the candidate wording does not establish the rainwater interpretation or potable/non-potable market mix.

## Business model and customer segments

Potential customers include rural/off-grid homeowners, drought- or water-restricted households, farms/ranches, commercial facilities seeking stormwater or non-potable reuse, builders and landscape/irrigation contractors. An installer might sell tank only, a complete gutter-to-tank system, or design/installation for potable domestic supply. Products include above-ground metal/poly tanks, buried concrete cisterns, modular storage, pumps, filters, disinfection and controls. Small tank installers can work on a single yard/irrigation system; potable or commercial systems may require registered plumbing/design professionals, engineering, inspection and multiple trades. Regional rainfall, water rights, climate/freezing, utility dependence, roof material and use case substantially change scope.

## Terminology and durable records

Capture site/catchment area and material, annual rainfall assumptions, intended demand/use, tank capacity/type/material/location, foundation/pad/soil/burial, gutter/screens/first-flush, inlet/filter, overflow/discharge, pump/pressure tank, treatment/disinfection, controls/level sensor, backflow/cross-connection measures, distribution zones, plumbing/electrical, permit/inspection, water test, model/serial, maintenance and warranty. The tank/system and pumps/filters persist; roof area and design yield are project inputs. Mark potable vs non-potable use prominently; store water testing and service history with date/lab/standard. Confined-space entry and water quality details may be safety-sensitive.

## Services, intake, quoting, and booking

Intake asks new vs repair/upgrade, property, roof/catchment, approximate demand, intended end uses, potable/non-potable, tank capacity/location, above/below grade, overflow route, existing gutters/plumbing/power, treatment goal, freeze/soil/flood conditions, access/equipment, water authority/right and permit status. Site assessment is needed for roof yield, pad/excavation, drainage, delivery and utility. A proposal separates tank, engineering/design, site preparation, excavation/base, gutter and prefilter, tank placement, pump/pressure/treatment, plumbing/electrical, overflow, permits, commissioning and owner training. Changes in intended use may alter treatment/code and must trigger design review.

Flow: inquiry → site/catchment/demand assessment → use classification and design/permit review → quote and approval → pad/excavation/tank order → install collection/pre-filter/storage → pump/treatment/distribution and overflow → test/inspection/commission → maintenance/water-quality handoff. Online intake can gather roof/site photos but cannot decide potability or code compliance.

## Pricing and commercial model

Price varies by tank capacity/material, above/below ground, excavation/crane access, base, catchment/roof modification, overflow, pump/pressure, distribution, treatment, potable design, inspection, electrical/plumbing trades and region. Texas Water Development Board guidance explains buried tanks cost more from excavation and stronger reinforcement, and tanks can be combined for capacity; this is not a universal price. Operator pages invite consult/quote but no representative price series was found. Residential rainwater service price is not software WTP. No operator SaaS purchase testimony or actual spend evidence was identified.

## Recurrence, scheduling, dispatch, and routing

Schedule design/permit, tank fabrication/delivery, excavation or pad cure, crane/placement, downspout/gutter work, plumber/electrician, treatment, inspection and test. Weather, ground conditions, site access, rainfall season, drought rebates and tank lead time affect date. Annual/seasonal maintenance, pre-filter cleaning, pump service or water testing can recur if offered; owner maintenance duties differ. Noncompletion: permit/right issue, tank access/grade, unsafe excavation, late tank, unsuitable roof/runoff, utility connection, overflow discharge, filtration/treatment hold or failed test. Track permit/quality hold independently of installation completion.

## Field workflow, safety, completion, and rework

Review design/ intended use and AHJ requirements → verify tank/support/soil/access → prepare base or excavation/secure tank → install collection screens/first-flush, inlet, overflow and venting → connect pump/pressure/treatment and distribution → protect potable supply with approved cross-connection measures where relevant → inspect/test for leak, overflow, pressure and system purpose → document water-quality test when required → label system and outlets as required → owner briefing/maintenance and signoff. EPA guidance and current state guidance show potable/non-potable requirements vary and cross-connection controls are critical. Heavy tanks, excavation/trenching, lifting/crane, electrical, confined-space and contaminated-water hazards need qualified controls. An installer checklist is not a substitute for registered design/plumbing or water-quality certification. Rework includes settling, leaks, overflow erosion, cross-connection, pump failure, poor water quality, freezing or inaccessible service parts.

## Customer communication and self-service

Customers need honest use classification, yield/capacity assumptions, site prep/delivery, outage, permit/inspection, treatment and test outcome, maintenance schedule, winterization, overflow routing, water-quality limits, warranty and service contact. Portal can expose manuals, serials, recent service and customer-readable test report. Do not label water potable or imply safe consumption without the relevant certified treatment, testing and local authority signoff.

## Equipment, inventory, suppliers, and workforce

Materials include metal/poly/concrete tank, gutters/downspouts, screens, first-flush diverter, filters, pumps, pressure tank, controls, UV/chlorine or other treatment as specifically designed, pipe, backflow devices, pad/bedding and overflow. Larger tanks require truck/crane or excavation equipment. Teams can include rainwater designer/installer, excavation contractor, plumber, electrician, roofer/gutter installer, water-quality lab and inspector. ASSE offers distinct installer, designer and inspector certifications; certification and licensing requirements are not universal. Track responsible professional and product model.

## Reporting and operating measures

Inferred: lead-to-site survey, design/permit turnaround, installed capacity, cost variance, delivery/installation cycle, pump/treatment commissioning pass, leak/water-quality call-backs, maintenance due/complete and gross margin by potable/nonpotable/irrigation/commercial scope. Capacity installed is not proof of water savings; actual yield depends on rainfall/use. No incumbent KPI standard found.

## Evidence, disagreements, and uncertainty

Rainwater installation requirements differ sharply by potable vs non-potable end use and jurisdiction. The EPA handbook is older and describes local adoption gaps at that date; it should not be used to assert current state rules. EPA state summaries and current ICC/ASSE resources are better for discovering requirements, but authoritative local code/AHJ review is still necessary. Operators demonstrate real projects in Texas/Ohio, not national prevalence. Software/WTP evidence is absent. Partial status reflects scope wording and market/software uncertainty.
