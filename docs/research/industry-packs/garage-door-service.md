## Executive summary

**Observed evidence:** Garage door businesses perform urgent repair/service (door stuck open/closed, broken spring/cable, off-track door, opener/sensor fault), planned maintenance, and replacement/new installation. A property may have multiple openings, each with a durable door/operator system, dimensions, weight/balance, track/spring configuration, opener/controls/sensors, model/serial, warranty, and service history. Dispatch intake affects safety, urgency, technician skill, and which spring/opener parts are loaded. DASMA/CPSC materials emphasize correct installation and testing of residential operator entrapment safeguards; an OSHA incident record documents a worker struck by stored spring force. ServiceTitan’s garage door product material and an owner/operator discussion indicate use of common FSM systems, with operator accounts describing both feature value and size/complexity fit concerns.

**Inference:** The strongest pack opportunity is one equipment record per opening, symptom-led triage, service-versus-install workflows, door/operator safety-test evidence, and a service-vehicle parts/purchase workflow that reduces return trips. Most CRM/scheduling/estimate/invoice needs map to shared capabilities; no garage-door-specific core gap is established. Full spring selection, door configuration, supplier catalog pricing, and commercial fire-door inspection rules need specialized tools or tenant-configured processes if applicable.

## Business model and customer segments

Residential homeowners make urgent repair calls and buy planned door/operator replacements or maintenance. Buyers also include landlords, property managers, builders, remodelers, self-storage, warehouses, retail, auto shops, and other commercial facilities. Triggers include a stuck door/security exposure, trapped vehicle, broken spring/cable, damaged panel/track, malfunctioning opener or photo-eye, storm/impact, noisy/imbalanced operation, move-in, remodel, aging equipment, or facility service schedule. A service call may be completed from truck stock or need a parts return; installation requires measurements, style/finish/insulation choices, lead time, delivery, removal, crew scheduling, and testing. Commercial overhead/rolling doors can require access coordination, multiple openings, larger crews/lifts, facility contracts, and compliance documentation. One/two-truck owner-operators may answer calls and work in the field; larger residential sales/service firms separate call taking, dispatch, sales, install crews, and warehouse; commercial specialists may have planned maintenance and account management.

## Terminology and durable records

Terms include customer/property, opening/door, sectional or rolling door, panel/section, track, rollers, torsion/extension spring, cable, drum, counterbalance system, operator/opener, photo-eye/safety sensor, remote/keypad, service call, repair, tune-up, replacement, install, quote/proposal, cycle rating, and callback. Store access and parking, opening location, clearances, door material/style/color/insulation, dimensions, verified weight/balance, track configuration, spring type/dimensions/cycle rating when recorded, operator make/model/serial, sensor/control/accessory details, warranty, prior repairs, and photos. Each distinct opening is a persistent asset; customer property/address alone cannot distinguish several doors. A spring measurement, impact damage, customer symptom, work performed, test result, and replaced part are visit/job data. Access codes and security-control details are sensitive; use least-privilege technician visibility and do not publish customer codes in notices/photos.

## Services, intake, quoting, and booking

Service jobs include diagnosis, spring/cable replacement, roller/hinge/track correction, off-track/reset, panel repair, opener/sensor/keypad troubleshooting, balancing, lubrication/adjustment, safety tests, and emergency securing. Sales/installation includes door replacement, opener/operator replacement, new opening installation, and commercial door work. Intake asks whether door is stuck open/closed, whether a car/person is trapped, visible broken spring/cable, door crooked/off-track, opener activity, photo-eye status, opening/property access, number of doors, make/model/photos if known, and whether commercial premises/security are affected. Dispatch can use this information to prioritize an unsecured building and stock likely parts, but customer self-diagnosis should not become a safe-to-operate promise or a definitive price.

The technician often inspects before final pricing because spring dimensions, door balance, opener compatibility, damage, and parts may be unknown. Repair may use menu/flat-rate work or a diagnostic/inspection followed by options; replacements use measured door specifications, options, materials, haul-away, labor, warranty, deposit, lead time, and install date. Commercial proposals may be site/asset-specific, bid, and contract-based. Customer approval should precede additional work; order-special/custom doors may need deposit and change/cancellation rules. Installation closeout should record fit/operation and operator safety-reversal/photo-eye tests where applicable, customer handoff, manuals, warranty, and permit/inspection result if local rules require one. Website booking can gather symptoms/photos and requested timing, but route unsafe or uncertain calls to office/technician review.

## Pricing and commercial model

Repair inputs include door/operator type, spring/cable/part dimensions, labor, stock availability, urgency/after-hours, travel, access, door size/weight, damage, and warranty. Replacement pricing depends on measurement, material/style/color/insulation/windows, track/hardware, opener/accessories, removal/disposal, structural changes, permit, crew and delivery. Commercial work can include site count, access/SLA, service agreement scope, inspection cycles, and parts pricing. Avoid a universal price formula: product types, supplier catalog/discount, regional labor, and option presentation differ. Service calls are mostly reactive, while commercial planned maintenance and warranty visits can recur; replacement is infrequent. Deposits, financing, payment at completion, warranty callbacks, maintenance discounts, and commercial net terms vary.

**Software willingness to pay:** A February 2024 garage-door subreddit thread contains a two-truck owner/operator stating they used Housecall Pro; it provides no verified subscription amount or explicit budget ceiling. A Feb 2025 self-described door-business owner said they had used Jobber, “loved Jobber,” outgrew it near $1M revenue, then moved to ServiceTitan when garage-door firms became eligible; the same post said they were not fully satisfied and were exploring alternatives. This is self-reported but has no disclosed subscription price. These are weak individual purchase/fit signals, not representative WTP evidence. Public plan prices are not proof of paid customer spend: Jobber’s official page advertised Core from US$29/month billed annually (accessed 2026-09-24; account/user limits vary); ServiceTitan displays per-technician plans but requires a quote/demo. No reliable garage-door-specific numeric WTP was found. Do not infer software budget from door replacement prices or revenue anecdotes.

## Recurrence, scheduling, dispatch, and routing

Residential work is mostly reactive, with optional periodic inspection/maintenance, warranty, and seasonal weather damage. Commercial facilities may schedule recurring inspections/service by opening and require priority response. Dispatch should capture urgency/security, door type/opening, likely parts, technician capabilities, truck inventory, customer window, building access, and whether the customer can safely leave the door alone. A call may be quoted after inspection, completed from stock, ordered for later return, or escalated to an install crew. New/replacement installations need site measure → quote/approval → special-order/delivery → remove/install → test/handoff sequence. Multi-opening buildings need separate assets but can group work by site. Routing benefits dense residential calls; parts readiness and skill can dominate distance. Allow emergency reprioritization, part backorders, return visit, weather reschedule, and customer status messages.

## Field workflow, safety, completion, and rework

Repair loop: collect symptom/urgency/access → assign trained tech with likely parts/tools → secure/inspect opening and identify door/operator → document condition and options → obtain approval → isolate/repair/replace → test balance/travel/reversal/sensors per applicable manufacturer instructions and requirements → capture replaced part, adjustment, photos, customer handoff and acceptance → invoice → warranty/follow-up. Installation adds measure/specification, proposal/deposit, special order, delivery, removal, install, operator setup, required safety tests, manuals/warranty, and permit/inspection if relevant. DASMA warns improper operator installation can create hazards and recommends demonstrating safety systems/manual release to owner; CPSC identifies residential operator requirements under 16 CFR Part 1211. OSHA records a worker struck by a spring-loaded bracket during garage-door work, underscoring stored-energy risk. CRM forms should record inspection/test outcomes and stop-work reasons, not teach torsion spring handling or assert compliance. Failure to complete may include unsafe/damaged opening, no access, wrong/missing part, incompatibility, special order, weather, structural issue, customer deferral, or failed test. Keep callbacks linked to original opening/job with workmanship/part/installation/customer-damage cause and warranty/billability.

## Customer communication and self-service

Useful messages include confirmation, symptom/safety guidance from approved company templates, ETA, delay, arrival, inspection findings/options, estimate approval, part-order/backorder timing, install schedule, access/security status, completed safety-test summary, operating/manual-release instructions, warranty/manual, invoice/payment, and follow-up. Customers may request repair/install, provide pictures, choose door/finish/options, approve/pay a quote, reschedule, and see service history. Commercial customers may need one contact for multiple facilities/openings, PO references, asset reports, and invoices by site. Avoid automated assurances that a door/opening is secure or safe before field verification; access control details and photos need visibility safeguards.

## Equipment, inventory, suppliers, and workforce

Service vans may carry common torsion springs, cables, rollers, hinges, brackets, bearings, remotes, photo-eyes, fasteners, opener components, winding/measurement tools, and safety equipment. Correct spring and door compatibility matters; technicians may need measurements and supplier lookups before committing stock. Inventory across vehicles, reserved parts, returns, obsolete models, and special orders can influence first-visit completion and return costs. New doors/operators are often made to specification or ordered through manufacturers/dealers; capture supplier, SKU/configuration, cost, expected arrival, and warranty serials where useful. Roles include dispatcher/call taker, door technician, installer/helper, salesperson/measure technician, warehouse/procurement, and commercial account technician. Training, manufacturer authorization, local contractor licenses, fall/lifting safety, and employer requirements vary. A certificate or skill tag may assist assignment, but the pack should not prescribe authorization rules. Time, commissions, spiffs, and payroll practices are operator-specific.

## Reporting and operating measures

Operator/vendor materials emphasize scheduling, estimates, parts and service history; useful measures include time to answer/book, response time for unsecured openings, first-trip completion, parts-related return rate, callbacks by door/part/technician, estimate conversion, repair-to-replacement conversion, average ticket by work type, warranty recovery, truck-stock turns/obsolete stock, install cycle and margin, commercial agreement visit completion, and receivables. First-trip completion and parts-return counts are plausible operating measures (garage-door trade publications discuss them), but no independent benchmark or normal value is established. Segment emergency, scheduled repair, replacement, and commercial installation reporting.

The most specific operator thread is a software-oriented door business owner who described starting with Jobber, outgrowing it around $1M revenue, and switching to ServiceTitan for sales tools, then considering alternatives because of dissatisfaction and gaps for owner/operator insight. In a garage-door CRM thread, one two-truck owner reported using Housecall Pro; the thread also mentions difficulty finding specialized options and product fit, though some replies contain promotional vendor claims and are excluded as independent evidence. These are individual accounts. Software stacks may also include QuickBooks, manufacturer/dealer ordering, messaging, spreadsheets, and dedicated door configurators/catalogs; direct evidence on prevalence is thin. ServiceTitan pricing is quote-based; no reliable current garage-door-specific paid price was found. Treat feature claims from vendors and owner reviews as separate evidence.

## Evidence, disagreements, and uncertainty

Sources agree on safety significance of operator installation/testing and on the business distinction between repair and installation. The limited operator evidence describes both smaller-shop interest in simple scheduling/invoicing and larger-shop interest in sales/dispatch/reporting depth; their reported transitions do not establish industry-wide software fit. DASMA/CPSC regulatory sources focus primarily on residential operators; commercial rolling/fire-door obligations require additional local and product-specific research. Confidence is **high** for per-opening equipment history, repair-vs-install distinction, and residential operator safety checks; **medium** for typical dispatch/stock workflow; **low** for recurring-plan prevalence, market share, software WTP, pricing models, and commercial compliance workflow. Unknowns include commercial vs residential share, manufacturer/dealer catalog coverage, actual part-return rates, and which software features buyers pay extra to obtain.

## Handoff to a future pack implementer

Prototype one asset per garage opening, service-address context, symptom/urgency intake, inspection and customer-approved options, repair/parts-return and installation/special-order flows, safety-test evidence, warranty/callback linkage, and optional truck-stock tracking. Ask residential/commercial ratio, number of openings/site, common door/operator types, service vs install work, emergency coverage, maintenance plans, parts/supplier setup, measurements/options, safety qualification, permitting, financing/deposits, and crew count. Keep pricing, spring/door selection, manufacturer integrations, warranty, safety procedures, commercial inspection obligations, and customer message templates configurable. Use current manufacturer instructions and applicable authority procedures; the CRM should retain outcomes rather than prescribe hazardous repairs.
