## Executive summary

**Observed evidence:** Pressure-washing contractors clean residential and commercial exterior surfaces, including concrete, building exteriors, storefronts, roofs, and turnover properties. Operators describe both scattered residential jobs and repeat commercial accounts; job scope and result expectations depend on surface, soil/stain, chemistry, equipment, and access. Field evidence emphasizes pre/post photos, expectation setting, documenting scope, and managing callbacks. EPA guidance adds a distinct runoff/containment concern when regulated renovation debris (including lead paint) is present; local stormwater requirements can also apply.

**Inference:** The durable record is the property with surface areas and prior treatment/condition history where useful; each quote/job needs a surface, approximate area, condition, access, chemical/process plan, and runoff context. A CRM pack can configure estimates, job evidence, site notes, and exception workflows. It should not prescribe one pressure, chemical, price formula, weather rule, or wastewater procedure. No cross-industry core gap is established by the reviewed evidence.

## Business model and customer segments

Residential owners request driveway, patio, siding, fence, deck, pool-area, and sometimes roof washing. Commercial buyers include storefronts, restaurants, multifamily properties, property managers, and construction/turnover contractors. A 2020 operator discussion describes a business with monthly commercial accounts, rental-turnover washes, and occasional residential work; another contractor describes store washing as a dense route add-on to window work. These examples show different business shapes, not market prevalence. Lead sources include local search/referrals, neighborhood marketing, web quote requests, and contractor/property-manager relationships. One-time work is common in the anecdotes; repeat contracts and annual/seasonal rebooking appear possible, especially for commercial properties and high-end residential clients. Weather, water access, surface type, travel, and seasonal demand bound the service area and capacity. Solo owner-operators, subcontractors, and multi-truck crews have different dispatch and equipment needs.

## Terminology and durable records

Use **customer**, **property/site**, **surface** (driveway/flatwork, siding, deck, roof, storefront), **wash/job**, **crew/technician**, **estimate**, **work order**, **pretreatment/post-treatment**, and **callback**. Site data may include address, property type, water-source location, access/parking, landscaping, outlets, fragile finishes, nearby drains, site contact/hours, and prior surface issues. Persist property and customer history; surface measurements, soil, stains, and requested scope can be quote/job inputs unless repeatedly used. Record surface material/finish, age/condition, prior sealant/coating, known damage, and restrictions where they change method or liability. Chemical/product, mix, label/SDS, PPE, runoff containment/disposal, and customer warnings may be safety/environmental records; retention and required detail depend on products, site, and jurisdiction. Separate private access notes from customer-visible scope and completion proof. Water source and actual wash areas are job-specific unless tracked as durable site features.

## Services, intake, quoting, and booking

Common menu: concrete/flatwork, driveway/patio, house wash/soft wash, storefront/building wash, decks/fences, pool enclosure, roof treatment, gum/oil/rust/stain treatment, post-construction or rental-turnover cleaning, sealing, and gutter/window add-ons. These are not universal. Intake should collect service address, requested surfaces, approximate dimensions, material/finish, height/access, condition/stains, water availability, nearby landscaping/drains, photos, desired timing, and whether sealing or specialty stain work is requested. Operators discuss estimating by area or standard service pricing, but challenge market-specific square-foot rates and note condition/surface differences; complex roofs, stains, coatings, and commercial scopes warrant review/site visit. Quotes should specify included surfaces, exclusions, stains not guaranteed removable, prep/chemical assumptions, water/runoff responsibilities, and add-ons. Most residential customers request an appointment/window; commercial sites may require off-hours access, recurring intervals, or tendered/project scopes. Website intake should collect photos and defer unusual surfaces, roofs, or regulated/contaminated runoff to staff.

## Pricing and commercial model

Evidence-backed quote inputs: surface type/material, measured or estimated area, height/access, soil/stain severity, chemistry/pretreatment, sealing, setup/travel, water and wastewater handling, obstacles/landscaping, commercial service window, recurrence, and minimum charge. Operator discussions disagree about area pricing versus standardized packages/hourly targets and cite geography and competition; these are individual accounts, not rate benchmarks. Quotes may be fixed or require inspection; commercial contracts can define periodic scope and access requirements. Deposits, cancellation, seasonal plans, warranty/callback and payment timing are tenant policy. **Software willingness-to-pay:** Opervo’s current gutter-focused price claim is not used here. Public general field-service price lists and contractor anecdotes do not establish a representative pressure-washing software budget. No independently verified operator purchase price was found; do not infer software budget from service quotes.

## Recurrence, scheduling, dispatch, and routing

Recurring work may include storefronts, restaurants, multifamily/common areas, rental turnovers, and annual residential maintenance; frequency is customer/site-specific. Residential projects may be route-scattered and low urgency; repeat commercial jobs can be clustered and scheduled at quiet hours. Weather, temperature, water availability, surface drying, and runoff constraints can reschedule work. Support estimate/site-visit stages, appointment windows, repeat service, route grouping, crew/equipment assignment, customer delay notices, partial completion, and return visits. Do not assume same-day completion for large commercial or multi-surface jobs. Route planning is conditional on route density/multiple crews; preserve manual sequencing and dispatcher overrides.

## Field workflow, safety, completion, and rework

Suggested lifecycle: review scope/site hazards and material restrictions; confirm customer access, water source, drain/runoff plan, and weather; document existing damage and before photos; protect plants/property and set containment where required; apply approved method/product to defined surfaces; inspect result and capture after photos; note inaccessible areas, remaining stains, damage, or extra work; walk through with customer/site contact or send completion update; collect approval/payment or schedule callback. Pressure, flow, chemical, dwell time, rinse, and access method must follow equipment/product/site requirements; the CRM should capture operator decisions, not issue technical instructions. OSHA ladder guidance applies when ladders are used; EPA Renovation, Repair and Painting guidance requires containment for covered renovation work and addresses wastewater; exact rule coverage depends on work and jurisdiction. Candidate exceptions: weather/unsafe access, water unavailable, locked site, scope mismatch, sensitive/failed surface, stain remains, runoff restriction, equipment failure, customer cancellation, or partial job. Operator forum reports show before/after documentation can help resolve quality disputes; remedies and guarantees vary.

## Customer communication and self-service

Useful messages cover quote follow-up, scope approval, water/access preparation, arrival/delay/weather, surface limitations, completion photos, invoice/payment, and seasonal maintenance reminder. A customer form can submit address/photos, surfaces and approximate size, preferred time, stain/condition, and water/access details. A portal may show estimate scope, before/after proof, invoice, and request follow-up. Have a person review chemical-sensitive surfaces, uncertain stains, roofs, lead-painted/regulated renovation work, runoff questions, and damage complaints. Avoid automatically promising full stain removal or a weather reschedule time.

## Equipment, inventory, suppliers, and workforce

Equipment may include pressure washers, surface cleaners, soft-wash/chemical application systems, hoses/reels, water tanks, pumps, ladders/lifts, PPE, containment/vacuum recovery, and vehicles/trailers. Chemicals, detergents, fuel, tips, hoses, and surface-specific products may be tracked by larger operators, but the evidence does not establish universal inventory control. Crew training and product/equipment familiarity matter; high-access roof work differs from ground-level concrete. Optional crew time, mileage, equipment assignment/maintenance, chemical/SDS records, and replenishment may help teams. No common piece-rate, certification, or payroll practice established.

## Reporting and operating measures

Potential reports: estimates sent/approved, revenue and labor by service/surface/site, route/travel time, repeat-account retention, callbacks/rework, jobs deferred for weather/access, photo completion, chemical/equipment usage, and commercial contract profitability. These measures are inferred as useful; reviewed sources do not establish shared KPI definitions. Keep safety/environmental event reporting distinct from revenue and production metrics.

General field-service platforms are a likely fit for estimates, scheduling, invoicing, payments, reminders, and crew dispatch, while the pressure-washing operator evidence foregrounds surface-specific quote inputs, proof photos, and exceptions. These forum accounts are anecdotal, sometimes old, and not representative samples. ## Evidence, disagreements, and uncertainty

Sources agree that scope and condition are highly job-specific and that quality documentation can help resolve disputes. Operators disagree on area pricing, hourly targets, and which surfaces or chemical services to offer. EPA guidance is authoritative but its lead-paint containment rule only applies to covered renovation work; local discharge controls require jurisdiction-specific research. Confidence is **medium-high** for quote inputs and job proof, **medium** for repeat commercial route workflows, and **low** for pricing norms, software budget, and market prevalence. Open questions include what share of operators capture wastewater, which municipalities impose specific discharge permits, and how often small crews use CRM software.

## Handoff to a future pack implementer

Prototype property/surface scope, photo-supported estimate and closeout, water/access/runoff notes, one-time jobs, optional recurring commercial work, and clear exception/callback handling. Ask surface mix, roof/high-access work, recurring account share, crew count, quote method, chemical/sealing services, and whether relevant jobs require runoff capture. Keep pricing, method, chemical records, service promises, and regulatory fields configurable. Validate with a solo concrete cleaner and a multi-crew commercial operator before setting broad defaults.
