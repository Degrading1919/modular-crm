## Executive summary

**Observed evidence:** Commercial cleaning firms sell recurring facility care under a scoped contract, often across areas and shifts, with periodic floor/carpet/window or deep work as separately scheduled services. Scope examples tie each task to a room/area, frequency, service window, access conditions, supply responsibility, and quality inspection. Janitorial software emphasizes site/employee schedules, checklists, inspections, communication, supply and time tracking. OSHA identifies chemical, equipment, and task-related worker hazards.

**Inference:** A pack should model client organizations, service sites, contract scope, area/task frequencies, crews, access/safety instructions, and inspection/callback workflows. The broad inventory label may also cover non-commercial or residential cleaning companies; this profile's strongest evidence concerns commercial janitorial contracts. Routine household maid services and intensive kitchen/restaurants require separate service boundaries and are not collapsed into this candidate.

## Business model and customer segments

Commercial cleaners sell recurring nightly/weekly services to offices, retail, multi-tenant buildings, healthcare, education, government, and other facilities through direct sales, property managers, procurement, and RFPs. Buyers may be facilities/procurement staff, property managers, or franchise/multi-site operators. Contracts vary from small single-site owner-operated teams to regional providers with supervisors, several crews, and multi-shift portfolios. Some work is subcontracted. One-time floor care, carpet extraction, post-construction, move-in/out, or specialty cleaning can complement recurring janitorial service.

Contract retention depends on reliable staffing, clear scope, access/security, quality checks, and response to complaints. Staffing may be scheduled outside business hours and across multiple shifts; a site manager may supervise while cleaners work independently. Residential cleaning has different customer access, quoting, repeat-cadence, and payment practices and is covered separately by Maid services.

## Terminology and durable records

Use customer/contract, facility/site, building/floor/zone/room, scope of work, task, service frequency, service window, cleaner/crew, supervisor, inspection, deficiency/callback, periodic project, and consumable. Durable site records include street address, facility type, square footage, floors/areas, access windows, key/badge/alarm procedures, security rules, site contacts, loading/parking, waste and supply locations, customer-provided vs contractor-provided consumables, floor/surface types, site-specific safety requirements, and contract scope/version. Access codes and security details should be sensitive staff-only data. Chemical Safety Data Sheets and employee training records are sensitive operational/compliance records; exact obligations depend on employer, task, and jurisdiction. Areas are durable service scopes, while spills, soil, and one-time obstructions are visit inputs rather than assets.

## Services, intake, quoting, and booking

Routine service may include restroom/breakroom cleaning, trash removal, vacuuming/sweeping/mopping, dusting, touchpoint cleaning, replenishment, and scheduled periodic floor/carpet/window tasks. Requirements differ for healthcare, food service, industrial, schools, offices, and public buildings; don't default regulated disinfection, kitchen hood cleaning, or restaurant deep cleaning into a generic janitorial pack. Intake should capture facility profile, cleanable area, use/occupancy, areas and task frequencies, service windows, current issues, access/security, supplies, staffing expectations, quality/inspection standards, insurance/background requirements, waste rules, and procurement deadlines. Site walkthroughs and RFP response are common when the site or scope is complex.

Quotes are often contract bids calculated from labor hours/productivity, frequencies, square footage or room/fixture counts, wage burden, supplies, equipment, shifts, access, travel, overhead, and periodic tasks. Flat monthly contract pricing and separately priced add-ons occur; exact method is operator- and buyer-specific. Website forms should collect facility/scope details and route complex or institutional bids to a walkthrough and review.

## Pricing and commercial model

Pricing inputs evidenced in commercial scope examples include facility/site area and type, room count, task frequency, service window, consumables, staffing, inspection/reporting, insurance/compliance, and periodic work. Contract prices can be fixed per month with defined extra-work rates; public procurements may require line-item bids. Service expansions, staffing changes, missed access, and client-requested extras need written scope/change control. Payment terms are contract-specific; public and enterprise customers may require purchase orders, invoice documentation, and renewal/tender periods.

**Software willingness-to-pay:** Janitorial Manager states its pricing is customized according to operation complexity and serves small crews through large/franchise operations; Swept publishes location-based plan packaging. These indicate commercial and scale-based software packaging but do not establish individual purchase price or broad willingness to pay. No sufficiently attributable operator software-spend statement was found; mark WTP unknown rather than infer from contract revenue.

## Recurrence, scheduling, dispatch, and routing

Service visits recur daily, multiple days per week, weekly, or on a customer-defined schedule, while periodic floor/carpet/detail tasks use separate cadences. Site opening hours, alarm/access rules, building zones, crew availability, language/skills, shift lengths, and customer disruptions affect assignment. Teams may serve one dedicated site or several sites per shift. Absences, late arrivals, badge/key issues, event closures, and access failures require supervisor reassignment or customer notification. Multi-site route sequencing is relevant to mobile crews, while large site crews can be site-based. Preserve the contract version and its schedule so a changed scope does not silently rewrite the historical service promise.

## Field workflow, safety, completion, and rework

Suggested lifecycle: review site scope/access/safety and assigned areas; check in; gather required tools/chemicals; complete tasks by zone and frequency; report blocked, unsafe, damaged, out-of-scope, or supply-short conditions; supervisor inspects according to contract; record checklist/inspection results and permitted photos; close visit and submit deficiencies; office communicates corrective action and tracks callback/resolution. Proof may be attendance, zone/task checklist, inspection score, incident note, supply replenishment, or customer sign-off depending on contract. OSHA warns of chemical, equipment, and injury/illness hazards and points to worker protections; cleaners should follow employer training, product labels/SDS, and site procedures. Don't prescribe one universal checklist or retention rule.

Noncompletion reasons include no access, alarm/badge failure, site closed, client area unavailable, unsafe/unknown substance, missing supplies/equipment, crew absence, time overrun, and weather for exterior work. Billability is contract-specific. Complaints, rework, deficient inspection, customer approval, and recurring corrective actions can affect contract retention; do not auto-close a complaint because a task checklist is complete.

## Customer communication and self-service

Clients need proposal/scope approval, onboarding and access coordination, service schedule, issue escalation, inspection findings, supply responsibility, periodic work notices, change-order approval, invoice and renewal communication. A facility contact portal may show site tasks, service issues, inspection outcomes, documents, and requests. Automated “completed” messages can be inappropriate if deficiencies require supervisor review or the contract excludes customer-facing proof. Secure access/key details; don't send badge codes in generic reminders.

## Equipment, inventory, suppliers, and workforce

Equipment may include vacuums, floor machines, carts, ladders, PPE, and site-specific tools; consumables may be contractor- or customer-supplied. Track inventory/replenishment where the contractor supplies products across many sites. Chemical product, dilution, SDS, and compatibility details can matter for safety, surface care, and procurement. Staffing includes cleaners, lead cleaners, site supervisors, floaters, and subcontractors; skills, training, background checks, attendance, shift assignment, clock-in/out, and payroll exports are conditional on contract/employment model. Avoid treating geographic tracking or individual performance surveillance as an automatic default.

## Reporting and operating measures

Janitorial incumbents advertise workforce, inspection, site, supply, and time oversight. Inferred useful measures: completed tasks/visits against scope, attendance/coverage, inspection/deficiency rate, callback resolution time, labor hours per site/area, supply use, contract margin, extra work, incident/safety follow-up, renewal/retention, and client issue volume. Report by client, site, area, shift, team, contract, task/service, and period. Scopes and inspection scores differ by customer, so avoid cross-contract score comparisons without normalization.

## Existing software and operator evidence

Janitorial Manager markets commercial-client/site management, inspections, employee schedules, time and supply tracking; Swept describes location-based pricing and cleaning-team operations. General field-service and office tools can be combined with QuickBooks, email/text, maps, time clocks, and spreadsheets. A commercial-cleaning owner has not been interviewed directly in the captured sample, but first-person posts from cleaning operators in adjacent segments report teams needing shared schedule/client notes, recurring jobs, reminders, and frustrations with bugs/support; another residential operator describes a stitched stack of scheduling software, Airtable, Make, Stripe, SMS, and QuickBooks for booking, billing, and client workflows. These adjacent-segment signals are not proof of commercial janitorial adoption.

The operator evidence is mixed: some small providers use calendars and texts; some move to a dedicated product at team scale; one operator describes a custom automation stack, while one user reports unresolved issues with their cleaning app. Commercial software pricing is often quote/custom or location-based rather than a single per-technician public price. Do not extrapolate the maid/residential software market to commercial contracts. Cleaning means many business models; here the operational configuration is commercial site cleaning and should not replace a separately configured household clean or deep kitchen.

## Evidence, disagreements, and uncertainty

Strongest direct evidence concerns commercial contract scopes and incumbent software descriptions; direct commercial-cleaning owner voice is sparse in this sample. Adjacent residential cleaning operator posts support only the broader observation that cleaners use mixed tools and value recurring schedules and communication. Facility types, supply responsibility, regulatory/customer requirements, task frequencies, and quality proof vary widely. The candidate could cover general residential cleaning too; Maid services and separate deep-kitchen profiles make this profile deliberately commercial/site-focused. Confidence is medium-high for task/site scope and contract recurrence, medium for software buying/workforce patterns, and low for prevalence of reported frustrations.

## Handoff to a future pack implementer

Prototype a client contract linked to multiple facility locations, a configurable area/task/frequency scope, access/safety notes, recurring visits, crew assignment, inspection and deficiency/callback tracking. Ask whether the operator cleans commercial sites, homes, or both; who supplies consumables; how sites/shifts/crews recur; which inspection proof clients require; and whether payroll/time, route sequencing, customer reports, or bid/tender intake matter. Keep safety rules, chemical practices, supply responsibility, task frequencies, and billability aligned to the tenant's contracts and jurisdiction.
