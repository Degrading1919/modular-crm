## Executive summary

Insulation contractors install fiberglass/mineral wool batts, blown cellulose/fiberglass and spray polyurethane foam (SPF) in attics, walls, floors, crawlspaces, roofs and commercial assemblies. The label combines materially different workflows. Standard batts/blown-in work is often measured by area, cavity, target R-value and bag coverage; spray foam requires specialized equipment, chemical handling, surface conditions, crew preparation, ventilation/re-entry restrictions and installed thickness/coverage evidence. Both require scope confirmation, material quantities, access/occupant coordination and verification that the work matches the specified assembly.

An insulation-company family operator describes hand-measuring attics/walls, calculating quantities and material pricing, labor and overhead, while identifying weak historical job-cost data; contractor replies mention setup/teardown, fixed walk order, line-item estimates and rigidity of FSM estimate templates. EPA warns that SPF chemicals such as isocyanates can harm workers and occupants and discusses ventilation/re-entry. The pack should keep spray vs standard methods distinct, support measured estimates, job/material tracking and evidence, and avoid automated safety, code or R-value certification claims.

## Business model and customer segments

Residential homeowners, remodelers, builders, multifamily owners and weatherization programs buy insulation; commercial/industrial work may serve GCs, developers, facilities and specialty envelope contractors. Job types include attic top-up, new construction walls/ceilings, retrofit dense-pack/blown-in, crawlspace/basement, sound insulation, air sealing and spray foam roof/wall systems. Companies may install material only, sell a building-envelope package, or subcontract for builders. Standard fiberglass/cellulose differs from open/closed cell foam in equipment, chemical exposure, thickness and cure controls.

Scale varies from owner/operator with one crew and rented/bought blower to SPF company with proportioning rigs, dedicated trucks, multiple applicators, estimators and safety program. New construction volume can be builder-driven and scheduled in sequence; residential retrofit tends to need inspection, occupant preparation and tailored quote. Weather, cavity access, ventilation, temperature and other-trade readiness affect productivity.

## Terminology and durable records

Record customer/project/site, building area, assembly/location (attic, wall, crawlspace, roof), square footage, cavity depth, existing material/condition, target R-value/spec, material/product/batch, thickness, coverage, air-seal detail, work zone, equipment/crew, completion evidence and warranty. Site fields include address, building age, access/hatch, occupancy, ventilation, wiring/obstructions, moisture, air sealing and work clearance. Building envelope areas may support future service history, but measurements, existing insulation and installed thickness are job-specific records. Preserve estimate and R-value assumptions, photos, product labels/batch, daily setup/weather/temperature where product requires, receipts and any inspection/permit record.

## Services, intake, quoting, and booking

Intake needs retrofit/new construction, assembly/area, measured square footage, existing insulation and condition, access, desired performance/energy audit, plans, product/method preference, occupancy, construction stage, electrical/fire/venting constraints and schedule. Site review is important for retrofit because actual area, access and hidden conditions affect price. Estimate by area, thickness/R-value, product coverage, bags/board feet, removal, air sealing, prep, equipment setup/tear-down, access, travel, disposal and site protection. For spray foam, include substrate/ambient/product temperature limits, masking, ventilation, re-entry plan, safety gear, and licensed specialty work if applicable. Maintain alternate material tiers and clear exclusions.

Schedule assessment/measure, prep/removal, install crew, builder/other trade readiness, ventilation and reoccupancy. Material order, machine capacity, chemical delivery/storage and waste removal influence field timing. Changes for blocked cavity, moisture, hidden wiring, framing or actual thickness/area should be documented before additional work.

## Pricing and commercial model

Inputs include area, thickness/target R-value, assembly, product density/coverage, material cost, crew hours, setup/tear-down, equipment, removal, air sealing, access, height, occupancy protection, travel and mobilization. Residential work may be quoted per area or package; builder/commercial work may use bid schedules, plan takeoff and progress billing. Changes arise from quantity variance, substrate, concealed conditions or product choice.

Operator sources describe material and labor estimating and actual-vs-bid tracking gaps but no dollar WTP. Jobber's public help explains job costing features are plan-gated, while its exact price varies by plan; this is incumbent packaging, not an insulation contractor's spend. Vendor comparison pages quote subscription figures but are not treated as operator evidence. Budget sensitivity and willingness to pay remain uncertain for one-truck firms vs multi-rig operators.

## Recurrence, scheduling, dispatch, and routing

Installation projects are typically one-time; builder relationships create repeated work and energy/repair projects may recur. Plan route/crew based on location, method and machine. Standard insulation jobs can be short; spray jobs require rig mobilization, preparation, ventilation and re-entry separation. Coordinate other trades, occupancy, inspection, temperature/weather and crew qualifications. Noncompletion reasons include site not ready, access, wet substrate, unsuitable conditions, product shortfall, equipment failure, ventilation/occupant issue or permit/inspection. Record affected area and customer/builder notice.

## Field workflow, safety, completion, and rework

Preflight confirms approved method/area, product, existing condition, access and occupant safety; prepare/cover zone; remove or protect where scoped; seal/prep; install by cavity/area and thickness; record product/lot/coverage; clean; photograph and verify agreed scope; restore access and hand off re-entry guidance. EPA warns SPF application can expose applicators/occupants to isocyanates, aerosols and dust, with respiratory/skin/eye effects; ventilation, PPE, restricted re-entry and product instructions matter. OSHA rules and building code requirements depend on material, task, jurisdiction and assembly; installer records do not replace inspection/certification.

Evidence includes before/after photos, area and thickness, product/batch, coverage/bag count, work zone and date, masking, existing condition, customer notice and exceptions. Rework may stem from missed areas, insufficient depth, gaps/voids, overspray, poor adhesion, odor/off-ratio foam, moisture, blocked vents or occupant access before safe re-entry. Stop-work and remedy require trained human judgment.

## Customer communication and self-service

Customers need assessment appointment, estimate assumptions, prep/clearance, work duration, occupants/pets protection, re-entry/ventilation notice for SPF, change scope, completion evidence, invoice and warranty. Online intake can collect building photos/plans and area estimates; final quote and safety instructions need provider review. Do not send generic re-entry duration without manufacturer/system-specific basis.

## Equipment, inventory, suppliers, and workforce

Materials include batt rolls, blown-in bags, foam components, sealants, baffles, mesh and protective supplies. Track material and product lot by job, truck stock and machine allocation where useful. Equipment includes blowers, hoses, SPF proportioner/rig, spray guns, lifts, respirators, ventilation and monitoring devices. Crew may include estimator, installer/driver, SPF applicator/lead, helper, safety/competent person and builder coordinator. Training/certification can be product/contract-specific; verify jurisdiction and product program.

## Reporting and operating measures

Operators identify actual-vs-estimate, setup/tear-down time, material quantity, labor hours and overhead as useful cost controls. Other inferred metrics: installed area/hour by method, material variance, rework/return rate, utilization, on-time completion, lead conversion and gross margin by assembly/product. Segment spray vs standard, retrofit vs new build, residential vs commercial and crew/rig. Avoid comparing production without thickness/access context.

## Existing software and operator evidence

Small insulation teams may use a unit-price sheet/spreadsheet plus general job scheduling/invoicing; residential estimators mention Jobber/Housecall Pro but say one-off estimation may be rigid. Jobber documents time/material job-costing capabilities and plan gating. Insulation software vendors market attic measurements, good/better/best R-value quotes, inventory and crew workflow, but claims and comparative pricing are vendor/editorial, not proof of actual usage. No independent subscription WTP was found. The best operator evidence is from a family business estimator taking over residential/new-build operations; one account cannot establish prevalence.

## Handoff to a future pack implementer

Ask standard vs SPF mix, new-build/retrofit split, assembly/area calculations, rig/crew count, job costing and inventory habits. Offer separate method templates and product-specific fields, plus occupant preparation and completion evidence. Keep exact R-value, code, safety and re-entry procedures under current product/contract rules; do not certify through CRM. Validate WTP with both small retrofit shops and builder-volume insulation firms.
