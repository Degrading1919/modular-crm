## Executive summary

**Observed evidence:** The shared Sweaty Startup article explicitly promotes a thermal imaging or home-inspection startup aimed at homebuyers and utility savings, with flyers, a report, realtor/referral leads, later commercial scanning and expansion into building inspection. It proposes equipment and revenue figures, but those are dated, unverified entrepreneurship advice rather than current market evidence. Two operational products sit under that umbrella: (1) a real-estate transaction inspection, generally a non-invasive visual examination on a specific date, where thermography may be a tool or paid add-on; and (2) a home-energy/building-envelope audit, which assesses energy performance and may combine IR with blower-door and other diagnostic measurements. IR detects surface temperature patterns; it does not alone diagnose hidden defects or predict utility bills.

A shared pack can configure property, building zones, observations, thermal images, conditions/limitations, report delivery and follow-up while offering two guided scopes. Keep the report and claim language distinct: transaction inspection findings are not an energy-savings calculation, and a home-performance audit is not a general property-condition certification. ASHI inspector interviews show varied packaging (included, fee add-on, separate scan) and stress interpreting IR as fact-finding with verification. No evidence supports one universal default price, credential, service promise or core capability gap.

## Business model and customer segments

**Home inspection path:** Buyer/seller, real-estate agent referral, investor or property manager books a whole-home inspection before purchase/listing/renovation. IR can be included in base inspection, paid add-on or limited separate scan. Timing follows transaction contingencies, access and contract deadlines. The inspector generally reports observed condition/limitations, not future performance, exhaustive hidden conditions or utility savings. Market differentiators include sample report, professional reputation, availability and transaction deadlines.

**Utility/energy-analysis path:** Homeowner, landlord, building owner, weatherization program or retrofit contractor requests energy-loss/envelope assessment, comfort/air-leak diagnosis or project documentation. A qualified audit may evaluate envelope, HVAC, electrical and appliances and use blower-door, infrared, pressure/combustion safety and other data. Utility incentive/program audits may have eligibility, credential and reporting criteria. Customers may pursue retrofit recommendations, but actual modeled savings depend on building, behavior, fuel rates, weather and proposed measures. These are service-trigger distinctions, not separate article origins.

Solo inspectors commonly combine field inspection and report writing; small multi-inspector firms may have schedulers, report QA, marketing and multiple territories. Energy audit firms may employ certified auditors/technicians and coordinate weatherization contractors/programs. Commercial thermography can involve roofs, electrical equipment or larger building envelopes and requires distinct qualifications, risk, instruments and contracts; it is not assumed in the homeowner pack.

## Terminology and durable records

Terms include client, buyer/seller, agent, property/site, structure, building envelope, room/zone, thermal image/thermogram, temperature differential, anomaly, moisture meter verification, air leakage, insulation void, thermal bridge, inspection limitation, energy audit, blower-door test, pressure reading, combustion safety, measure/recommendation and report. Property/location records may include address, building type/age/area, occupancy, transaction role/contacts, access, construction type, floor plan, service scope, inspection date, weather/indoor-outdoor temperature conditions and safety/access restrictions.

Persistent property history may retain prior inspection/audit/report and known systems/envelope improvements, with permission and clear date/scope. One-job records include sensor/camera, calibration/configuration as applicable, surface/material, room/location, emissivity/reflected temperature notes where used, camera image, matching visual photo, measurement reading, differential/environmental conditions, verified follow-up tool, observation, limitation, recommendation and client acknowledgement. Thermal images can reveal private property details; report sharing needs explicit recipient/retention controls. Camera and blower-door instruments are company equipment, not customer assets.

## Services, intake, quoting, and booking

Two service forms should remain distinct:

1. **Inspection with optional IR:** homebuyer/pre-list inspection of accessible components; IR used to check temperature patterns and direct further inspection, alongside standard inspection findings. Intake needs purchase/listing deadline, property size/age/type, occupancy, known leaks/comfort concerns, requested scope, access, visible conditions, optional ancillary services and report deadline. Home inspection has standards-defined exclusions and is based on observations that day.
2. **Energy/envelope assessment:** scoped whole-house audit or diagnostic visit, potentially with blower-door/pressure/combustion tests and energy-use/bill information. Intake needs client goal, utility/fuel data if relevant, building/zone data, occupancy, requested testing, renovation/weatherization program, access, known combustion appliances and whether client needs an audit report or diagnostic screening. Program-funded audits must meet program rules.

Quote may be based on base property inspection price adjusted for square footage, age, units, travel and transaction needs; IR inclusion/add-on; or a separately scoped audit by size/tests/diagnostics/report and credentials. A thermal scan alone may be limited/fixed scope. Report should state method, observations, limitations, corroborating test and what further qualified investigation is recommended. Web intake can book standard inspection windows; complex audit/program eligibility needs review.

## Pricing and commercial model

Home inspections are commonly sold per property with adjustments for size/age/units/travel and optional tests; operator practices vary. Energy audits may be customer-paid, program-funded or contractor/utility-arranged, with scope and approved amounts set by program. Thermal-image count or camera use alone is not a defensible measure of audit value. Home inspection customers need clear written scope, payment/cancellation deadline, exclusions and report delivery. Avoid quoting utility savings from a thermogram absent an appropriate analysis and assumptions.

**Observed software list price/WTP boundary, accessed 2026-09-24:** HomeGauge's official pricing page lists $89/month after its 30-day trial, and Spectora lists $109/month after two free inspections (check current plan terms). These are software prices, not actual home inspector payments or evidence of what energy auditors purchase. Inspector discussions report service packaging disagreement: an ASHI article quotes operators who charge an add-on (from $25 to 50% above inspection price), others include IR and find a separate thermal offering has too little demand. A 2023 buyer thread reports one specific $500+ inspection and $150 infrared add-on; it is not a representative fee survey. The Sweaty Startup article's “$150+ per service” statement is unverified marketing advice, not a current service-price baseline. No independently observed software invoice or representative WTP study found.

## Recurrence, scheduling, dispatch, and routing

Transaction inspections are generally one-time; a buyer may order separate inspections for multiple properties, or owners may request follow-up after repair. Energy audits are usually event-driven, sometimes repeated after retrofit/program phases, not automatically annual. Thermal conditions matter: temperature contrast, solar loading, wind, weather, HVAC operation, surface type and time of day can change interpretability. Schedule adequate conditions and clarify limitations; do not promise thermographic results under unsuitable conditions. Home inspection windows align with buyer access/transaction deadline and often a several-hour site visit plus report writing. Energy audits can require prep instructions, occupants, blower-door setup, combustion safety and more time. Commercial/portfolio routes are conditional. Missed access, unsuitable thermal/weather conditions or unsafe combustion conditions may require reschedule/referral.

## Field workflow, safety, completion, and rework

**Home inspection:** lead and scope agreement → review disclosures/known concerns → confirm access/utility status → visual walk-through and permitted tests → capture thermal/visual pairs at relevant locations → verify suspicious moisture with appropriate meter/inspection method → document limitation and referrals → report findings on observed condition, not prediction → review/deliver report, answer questions, invoice/close.
**Energy audit:** establish client goal and program eligibility → review building/utility information → assess conditions and combustion risks → perform audit protocol (envelope/HVAC/other systems) and tests such as blower-door where applicable → use thermal imaging under appropriate conditions → interpret alongside other measurements → document measures/priority, assumptions and limitations → QA/report → client/program review and optional contractor handoff.

InterNACHI defines home inspection as a non-invasive visual examination of accessible areas, a dated observation that does not find all concealed defects. Standards limit predictions and specialized determinations. Thermal anomalies are temperature differences, not proof of moisture, air leakage, missing insulation or electrical fault; sunlight, reflection, thermal bridging, wind, HVAC status and material can create misleading patterns. Verify with moisture meter, visual evidence or appropriate qualified test. DOE materials use IR among building-envelope diagnostics; BPI standards define home energy auditing and quality practice. Energy auditors may also need combustion safety testing; follow qualified program/standard practice, not an IndustryPack-generated checklist. Credentials are program/association-specific; state licensing varies. Noncompletion reasons include no access, utilities/HVAC off, unsuitable temperature differential, weather/solar loading, unsafe combustion, inaccessible system, client scope change or no test condition. Record limitation, customer notice, refund/reschedule, recommendation and who may follow up.

## Customer communication and self-service

Before visit: who the client is (buyer, seller, owner, program), inspection versus audit scope, access/utilities, occupant prep, report recipients, ancillary tests, weather/thermal limits and delivery deadline. During: explain observed anomaly versus diagnosis and obtain permission for additional testing. After: deliver report and image/visual pairs, identify limitations, answer questions and direct further evaluation to appropriate trade/professional. Do not promise lower bills, defect-free home, code compliance or hidden moisture detection from IR alone. Portal could handle scheduling, agreement, payment, property disclosures, report delivery and authorized sharing; do not expose seller/occupant data beyond consented recipients. Program data and reports may require additional consent or retention controls.

## Equipment, inventory, suppliers, and workforce

IR cameras vary in resolution, sensitivity, lens and measurement features; temperature calibration and understanding reflective surfaces matter. An energy audit may also use blower door/fan, manometer, pressure pan, duct blaster, combustion analyzer, flow/airflow measurement and moisture meter. The ASHI article discusses camera selection and training; the candidate article claims a camera under $350, but current professional requirements/prices should be verified before use. Equipment is a business asset requiring service/calibration and assignment, not part of customer inventory. Workforce may include home inspector, certified thermographer, BPI energy auditor/technician, QA reviewer, scheduler and partner contractor; one person can hold multiple credentials. Track certifications/expiry and insurance if relevant, but do not imply CRM status establishes competency. Travel, inspection duration and report-writing time dominate labor; payroll is conditional by company size.

## Reporting and operating measures

Useful measures differ by path. Home inspection: leads by source, conversion, inspections/day, report turnaround, ancillary attach rate, travel, referral repeat, correction/revisit, complaint/E&O claim and customer review. Energy audit: eligibility/booking, test completion rate, time per dwelling, QA corrections, recommendation category, retrofit follow-through when observable, program reimbursement and report turnaround. Savings realized should only be reported if measured/modelled under a stated protocol; do not count projected savings as actual. Incumbent inspection software markets report writing, scheduling, templates, online booking, payment and customer portal. KPIs above are suggested; the sources do not show universal operator dashboard practice.

## Existing software and operator evidence

Home-inspection tools include Spectora and HomeGauge for scheduling, onsite data, report-writing, client delivery, payments/marketing; energy auditing may use program-specific forms, spreadsheets, measurement devices and contractor/program systems. QuickBooks/accounting, calendars, maps, email/text, report/photo storage and association/member systems can supplement. Spectora's official materials advertise offline mobile app, reports and online workflows; HomeGauge differentiates cloud/mobile and desktop reporting. Those vendor pages show product positioning, not adoption by thermal-imaging specialists.

ASHI first-person inspector accounts conflict on whether IR is free differentiation, included in a higher base fee, an add-on, or an independent service. Two interviewees in ASHI's article explicitly report different outcomes: one regretted not charging for IR; another says demand for a separate IR service was insufficient. They caution use for fact-finding and verifying temperature anomalies rather than diagnosis. InterNACHI forum discussions similarly show inspectors debating extra time, fee and inclusion. WTP for inspection fees is distinct from software budget. Public software list prices are accessible, but operator software subscription purchases for this niche were not found.

## Evidence, disagreements, and uncertainty

The same candidate article URL is confirmed for both inventory labels; its headline and content combine a home inspection company and utility-saving thermal scan, making shared coverage reasonable while retaining two workflows. Its equipment and $150-per-service claims are aspirational, unverified and likely date-sensitive. Professional sources are more careful: inspection standards define non-invasive scope and limits; DOE/BPI energy auditing treats IR as one tool among whole-house tests; ASHI operators disagree about charging separately. Thermal interpretation depends on conditions and trained judgment. Confidence is high that IR is a tool and not a standalone diagnosis or utility-bill forecast, medium on useful pack workflows, and low on market demand, service pricing and software WTP.

## Handoff to a future pack implementer

Use one profile/pack only if onboarding selects either or both service paths, then fork inspection and energy-audit forms, checklist and report language. Ask who buys (homebuyer, owner, program), whether IR is included/add-on/separate, audit credential/program, other instruments/tests, report recipients and deadline. Keep standards, licensing, test conditions, assumptions, pricing and report wording tenant-configurable; require a documented limitation/verification path for thermal anomalies. Do not promise utility savings from thermal images or merge a home inspection with energy audit certification. Unresolved questions: whether paired image/visual annotation is a common platform need or belongs in inspection-report integrations; local licensing/credential matrix; observed software spend by inspectors and auditors; and how often consumers will pay separately for IR.
