## Executive summary

**Observed evidence:** Electrical contractors range from owner-operated residential service shops to commercial/industrial contractors bidding multi-trade construction projects. Service calls diagnose faults, restore power, replace devices, and address urgent hazards; planned work includes panel/service upgrades, generators, EV charging, rewires, tenant improvements, and new construction. The latter needs estimating, materials/labor takeoff, schedule coordination, purchase orders, change orders, job costing, permits, inspections, and closeout; a service ticket and a construction project are distinct operating paths. OSHA identifies shock, electrocution, fire, and explosion hazards, and Wisconsin’s state electrical authority routes some permit and inspection matters to delegated municipalities.

**Inference:** A pack is useful for switching between service and project intake, keeping property/panel/circuit/equipment history, collecting photos and customer approvals, and recording permits/inspection outcomes without asserting code compliance. Shared CRM, field job, estimate, scheduling, payment, and accounting functions cover much of the workflow. This research does not establish an electrical-only core gap; full construction estimating and project controls may exceed the documented IndustryPack contract, but evidence is insufficient to claim that the shared product cannot represent them.

## Business model and customer segments

Customers include homeowners and tenants, landlords/property managers, general contractors, builders, facility managers, commercial businesses, and public/institutional buyers. Work may arrive by phone, referrals, web lead, service agreement, contractor bid list, or construction plan/specification. Triggers include outage, tripping breaker, damaged wiring, failed equipment, inspection finding, remodel, tenant turnover, generator/EV/solar integration, and new construction. Emergency response and on-call work may be priced or staffed differently. Residential service can close in one visit or require a quote/parts return; commercial and construction work commonly spans multiple visits and trades. Solo electricians combine office/field work; larger firms divide dispatch, service, estimating, project management, and crews. A service-focused electrical company is not equivalent to an electrical subcontractor focused on bid-build projects.

## Terminology and durable records

Common terms include customer/account, service address/site, panel/service, circuit, device, equipment, service call/work order, deficiency, estimate/proposal, takeoff, bid, change order, permit, inspection, punch list, and closeout. Store property access, occupancy/contact, panel location and identification, known service rating when verified, prior work, photos, utility/tenant constraints, and site-specific safety notes. Durable assets can include panels, generators, transfer switches, EV chargers, lighting/control systems, and other maintainable equipment; circuit labels, fault readings, materials, and temporary conditions usually belong to job history. Never treat an electrician-entered value as an engineering determination or code approval. Access codes, energized-equipment hazards, and staff-only safety notes should be access-controlled; distinguish technician notes from customer-visible reports.

## Services, intake, quoting, and booking

Service categories include troubleshooting, repair, outlet/switch/lighting work, panel/service work, generator and transfer-switch service, EV charger work, safety/inspection corrections, and maintenance. Project scopes include rough-in/finish, tenant fit-outs, remodels, service upgrades, and new construction. Intake needs symptom and affected area, urgency, loss-of-power/smoke/burning/visible-damage cues, property/customer authority, address/access, photos if safe, existing panel/equipment details, and desired timing. Triage urgent hazards to human review; do not use automated diagnosis or promise that a site is safe.

Smaller service tasks may use a diagnostic/trip charge, flat-rate pricebook, time-and-materials, or a quote after inspection. Upgrades and construction jobs may use site walks, plans, labor/material takeoff, alternates, exclusions, allowances, schedule, deposit/progress billing, and change orders. Electrical estimating software discussions distinguish commercial estimating databases from a flat-rate book used for service (Mike Holt forum, anecdotal). Obtain written customer approval before chargeable work beyond the original scope. Permit applications and inspections are tied to local rules and authorized parties; record authority, permit number/status, inspection date/outcome, and corrections only when applicable. Online booking should gather issue and property context while routing emergencies, unclear scopes, and bid work to staff.

## Pricing and commercial model

Pricing inputs vary by scope: labor/crew hours, material and equipment cost, access/height, diagnostic uncertainty, urgency, travel, code-required upgrades, permit/inspection fees, schedule risk, subcontractors, and overhead. Flat-rate/menu and time/material approaches are plausible for service; measured bids and unit/assembly takeoffs are more suited to construction. Customer deposits, progress invoices, retention, change orders, warranties, and payment terms are company and contract specific. Keep formulas, markups, tax, and approval thresholds tenant-controlled.

**Software willingness to pay:** A May 2024 r/electricians post from a self-described residential new-construction contractor with 12 field employees and one office employee reported demo quotes of roughly $1,500/month for ServiceTitan, $600/month for FieldPulse, and $300–400/month for Jobber; this is one unverified, dated buyer report, not a market rate or confirmed purchase. A March 2026 Mike Holt forum participant, identifying as a Chicago electrical contractor doing $12–15M annual revenue, said Accubid Classic was quoted at $2,200 per user and discussed needing three licenses; this is a commercial estimating-tool price signal, not CRM willingness-to-pay. The Housecall Pro electrical page advertised plans and, as accessed 2026-09-24, stated individual tiers at $79/$229/$399 per month and team Basic from $79, Essentials $299 for five users, MAX $649 for eight users; vendor list pricing is not proof of actual spend or willingness to pay. Jobber publishes plan pricing by business size; its public industry page describes Home Depot price/inventory integration. ServiceTitan requests pricing via demo. Do not infer CRM budget from project values.

## Recurrence, scheduling, dispatch, and routing

Most residential repair is triggered work, with repeat history and possible warranties; preventive maintenance and commercial service agreements create scheduled recurrence. Seasonal storms, heat/cold demand, outages, and construction phases may affect capacity. Dispatch matches urgency, trade/license/skill, geography, crew size, parts/tools, site rules, and expected duration. Project scheduling needs milestones, crew continuity, other-trade dependencies, delivery timing, inspections, and return/punch work. Support exact appointments or arrival windows for service, on-call escalation, multi-day blocks for projects, schedule changes, and customer notice. Route optimization is secondary to skill, emergency priority, and work sequencing for many electricians.

## Field workflow, safety, completion, and rework

Service loop: capture the reported condition → triage hazards/authority → schedule appropriately qualified staff → inspect and document existing condition → quote options and receive approval → isolate/repair/install and record materials → perform required tests → obtain inspection where applicable → capture completion notes/photos/labels and customer acceptance → invoice/collect → follow up. Project loop adds bid and scope approval, permit/plan coordination, procurement, rough-in/inspection, finish, punch list, change orders, and closeout documents. OSHA highlights electric shock, electrocution, fire, and explosion hazards; no app checklist substitutes for safe work practices, training, PPE, or applicable law. Record energized status/hazard or stop-work/escalation when relevant, but do not turn a general CRM into a safety procedure. Noncompletion includes unsafe condition, no access/authority, utility interruption, missing material, failed inspection, scope change, and customer deferral. Track return reason, warranty/callback, original job, and billability separately.

## Customer communication and self-service

Useful communication includes booking confirmation, arrival window/on-the-way, delay, findings and options, approval request, outage/access preparation, permit/inspection coordination, completion/test summary, invoice/payment, and warranty/follow-up. Customers may request service, share panel/equipment photos, approve quotes, pay, and see approved documents. Commercial buyers may require COI/vendor onboarding, PO, site contact, invoice routing, lien/closeout documentation, and scheduled access. Human review is required for hazard reports, uncertain conditions, scope changes, and any claim about code compliance or safety. Make SMS/email consent and sensitive image visibility configurable.

## Equipment, inventory, suppliers, and workforce

Typical job materials include wire/cable, breakers, boxes, conduit, devices, fittings, panelboards, lighting, generators, transfer switches, and job-specific equipment; quantities can be small truck stock or project procurement. Track committed/used/returned material and purchase orders when useful; do not assume every solo operator needs serialized inventory. Workforce may include licensed electricians, apprentices, helpers, estimators, project managers, and subcontractors. License class, continuing education, permitted supervision, and certification rules vary by jurisdiction and work scope. Capture skills/credentials/expiry for assignment support, but let employers and local authorities define qualifications. Project teams may require time allocation and job costing; business-specific pay, union rules, prevailing wage, and payroll exports remain configurable.

## Reporting and operating measures

Incumbent pages advertise revenue, job costing, estimate conversion, technician performance, and job/checklist history. Candidate measures include response time, first-visit completion, callback/rework by cause, estimate approval/conversion by service/project type, labor/material variance, gross margin, permit/inspection correction rate, project milestone variance, warranty claims, and outstanding receivables. Definitions and validity vary; never compare service tickets with project bids without segmentation.

## Existing software and operator evidence

Small service operators mention Jobber, Housecall Pro, Workiz, FieldPulse, and QuickBooks; project estimators discuss Accubid, McCormick, ConEst, and similar estimating tools. This suggests service CRM and detailed bid estimating may be separate systems for some firms, not a universal stack. Housecall Pro documents customer/property/equipment history, quotes, attachments, job checklists, and customer messaging; Jobber markets service scheduling, quotes, and supplier price lookup; ServiceTitan presents pricebook, estimates, dispatch, project and field functions but requires a demo for price.

Operator discussions are mixed and self-selected. In May 2024, one 12-field-employee new-construction electrical contractor described Jobber as basic, FieldPulse as a middle option, and ServiceTitan as feature-rich but far more expensive; the same post described uncertainty and fear of migration regret. In a March 2026 Mike Holt commercial estimating thread, a contractor raised license limits and doubling cost in a move from Bidwinner to Accubid Classic, while respondents discussed configurable databases, training, and job-costing handoffs. A May 2024 Mike Holt owner said ServiceTitan’s price/user fees seemed excessive. These are useful friction signals, not representative prevalence estimates.

## Evidence, disagreements, and uncertainty

Sources agree on a service/project split, need for estimates and customer/site history, and the safety/permit significance of electrical work. They show different software tiers: simpler FSM tools for smaller service crews and dedicated estimating systems for commercial bids. Operator accounts disagree on whether deeper systems justify their cost and implementation burden. Confidence is **high** for the distinct service vs construction paths and for safety/jurisdiction variation; **medium** for common customer/asset/workflow fields; **low** for typical service/project mix, recurring-plan penetration, and software budget. Unknowns include the candidate’s segment, jurisdictions, whether it performs residential service or subcontract work, and which project functions exceed configurable workflows.

## Handoff to a future pack implementer

Prototype separate service and project flows, a property/panel/equipment record, on-site findings/options/approval, material usage, configurable permit/inspection fields, completion/test evidence, and callback links. Ask about residential vs commercial/new construction, emergency work, team/licensing structure, estimating and accounting tools, maintenance agreements, permits, materials, deposits/progress billing, and customer booking. Keep local compliance, pricing, qualifications, authorization, and warranty rules tenant-controlled. Validate project dependencies/takeoff/progress billing against actual shared product functions before declaring a core gap.
