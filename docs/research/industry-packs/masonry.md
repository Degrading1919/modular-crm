## Executive summary

Masonry contractors build, repair or restore assemblies using brick, concrete masonry units (CMU), natural/manufactured stone and mortar. Work ranges from residential patios, chimneys, steps and veneer to commercial walls, structural/block scopes, restoration and public projects. Estimation relies on plans or measured area/length/counts, material selection, openings, bond/detail, access, scaffold, labor productivity, weather and wall reinforcement/grout requirements. Durable workflow centers on estimate/takeoff → material and crew plan → staged construction/inspection → field quantities and photos → progress/final billing and warranty.

MCAA provides estimating education and business/safety resources; IMI provides technical detailing resources. Operator discussions describe masonry estimators building per-block/productive labor costs and combining takeoff tools with Excel or estimating assemblies. This supports configurable service/assembly templates, not a universal masonry price or production rate. The CRM can cover customers, projects, scheduling, estimates, changes, field evidence and invoices; drawing/BIM interpretation and engineered design remain specialist tools/services.

## Business model and customer segments

Residential customers hire for new walls, veneer, chimneys, fireplaces, patios, walkways, retaining walls and repairs/tuckpointing. Commercial customers include GCs, developers, institutions, property managers and architects; public projects may involve bid forms, submittals, inspections and progress billing. The masonry contractor may be a prime for small work or a specialty subcontractor on a large building. Distinguish new construction, restoration, structural/engineered masonry, veneer and hardscape; equipment, codes, inspection and quote methods differ.

Jobs can be fixed-price repair visits, measured residential projects, negotiated unit prices or competitive commercial bids. One-person repair businesses have office-light intake and owner scheduling; larger wall contractors employ estimators, PMs, foremen, multiple crews, scaffold/equipment and material procurement. Seasonal weather, freeze protection, curing and site readiness shape the calendar.

## Terminology and durable records

Store customer, project/site, building/elevation/area, contract/GC, plan/spec revision, estimate/takeoff, assembly, material/finish, crew, work phase, daily report, inspection, change order, punch and warranty. Site data may include address/access, wall/elevation/height, substrate, existing construction, dimensions/openings, staging/scaffold, water/drainage, adjacent finishes, weather exposure and occupied-site limits. A building/wall can be a persistent asset for restoration history; measured quantities, mortar mix and material lots are job-specific.

Retain marked drawings, elevation/photos, takeoff measurement, material and color selection, mortar/assembly detail, reinforcement/grout assumptions, approved submittals, delivery tickets, weather and protection notes, daily installed quantity, inspection evidence and signed changes. Do not infer structural suitability or engineering approval from a CRM record.

## Services, intake, quoting, and booking

Intake asks whether new construction or repair/restoration, material/type, area/linear footage/count, height/access, drawings/photos, openings/details, existing damage, finish/color match, structural concerns, water intrusion, schedule, buyer/GC, permits and inspection requirements. On-site inspection is common for repairs and complex work. Estimates may price square feet, linear feet, unit counts, assembly/labor hours or time/material. Include brick/block/stone, mortar, sand, reinforcement, grout, ties/anchors, flashing, scaffold/lift, demolition/disposal, protection, access, mobilization, cleanup, weather measures, tax, overhead and contingency. Preserve assumptions/exclusions, alternates, drawing revisions and approved change orders.

Stages may include site measure, plans/takeoff, submittal/sample approval, procurement, scaffold/access, substrate readiness, layout, reinforcement/inspection, laying/grouting, curing/protection, cleanup, punch and acceptance. Coordinate delivery/storage, crane/forklift, GC sequencing, openings/embeds, flashing/waterproofing trades and inspection. Material/color lead time and crew skill can be significant; remeasure/change management prevents wrong product quantities and scope disputes.

## Pricing and commercial model

Price is driven by material/product, color/finish, pattern/bond, area and unit count, wall height/thickness, cuts/openings, reinforcement/grout, access/scaffold, restoration matching, demolition, disposal, weather protection, location, labor productivity and minimum mobilization. Residential work may use deposit/progress/final payments; commercial work may use schedule-of-values, pay applications, retainage, unit prices and approved change orders. Warranty terms vary by assembly/material and contract.

One estimator discussion reports building costs per block and accounting for productive rates, with STACK, OnScreen Takeoff and assembly-based estimating software mentioned. Another user reports a low-cost takeoff option at $39/month, but comments include a vendor/creator and do not establish market adoption. MCAA estimating instruction confirms the importance of bid documents, estimate methods and labor/material math, not willingness to pay. No independent operator statement about actual CRM spend was found; software budget likely differs between small repair masons and commercial subcontractors and remains uncertain.

## Recurrence, scheduling, dispatch, and routing

Most jobs are discrete projects; restoration, tuckpointing and property maintenance may repeat when inspected/needed. Scheduling depends on crew size/skill, scaffold/lift, material delivery, wall/GC readiness, inspection hold points, weather, freeze/heat protection and adjacent trade sequence. A crew may move between multi-day project sites, but routing is generally less central than resource and phase planning. Noncompletion causes include rain/freezing conditions, unsafe scaffold, missing material, access blocked, predecessor incomplete, failed inspection, concealed substrate conditions or customer/GC change. Record whether delay is billable/compensable per contract without automatic determination.

## Field workflow, safety, completion, and rework

Preconstruction drawing/spec review → site/layout check → staging/scaffold and substrate readiness → verify units/mortar/detail → build in lifts with reinforcement/grout/inspection as specified → protect/cure → clean/point/finish → quality review and punch → acceptance/closeout. Field evidence may include wall/elevation/phase, installed units or area, material lot/color, mortar, reinforcement/grout check, photos, weather/protection, scaffold inspection, delivery shortages, inspection release, nonconformance and change approval. MCAA resources include wall-bracing and safety topics.

OSHA's silica rule applies to covered construction work and lists cutting/grinding/drilling masonry among potential silica-generating tasks; use task-appropriate controls. OSHA construction scaffold rules, falling-object risks, manual material handling, silica, mortar/chemical contact and work at height matter. Licensing/engineer requirements depend on structural scope and jurisdiction. Rework includes color mismatch, poor bond/alignment, mortar joint defects, water penetration, cracking, out-of-plumb wall, failed reinforcement inspection or damage to adjacent work. Track corrective action, owner notice, photos, responsible party and warranty status.

## Customer communication and self-service

Customers need a clear scope/material/color, measure/site visit, bid status, schedule and weather updates, site preparation, inspection access, change approval, progress, punch completion, invoice and warranty/maintenance guidance. Collecting photos and appointment requests online is reasonable; final repair diagnosis, color match, structural scope and code interpretation need human review. Commercial document exchange should follow GC/owner process.

## Equipment, inventory, suppliers, and workforce

Materials include brick, block, stone, mortar/cement/lime/sand, grout, reinforcement, anchors/ties, flashing, sealants, scaffold components and protection. Track unit/type/color/lot, supplier delivery, breakage/shortage and job allocation where materially useful; small firms may buy to project and avoid full inventory control. Equipment includes mixers, saws with dust controls, scaffold, lifts, forklifts, mortar boards and specialty restoration tools. Teams may include masons, tenders/laborers, foreman, estimator/PM and specialist scaffold, engineer or testing subcontractors. Track time and productivity where used for job cost; piece-rate and union practices vary.

## Reporting and operating measures

MCAA estimating materials and operator accounts support labor/material estimating, while job-level useful measures inferred include takeoff-to-actual quantity variance, installed units/area per crew shift, material waste/breakage, labor hours per assembly, gross margin, schedule variance, inspection deficiency/rework, callbacks and receivables. Report by masonry type, material/assembly, crew, GC, project size and repair/new-construction segment. Avoid imposing one production target across brick, block, stone and restoration.

## Existing software and operator evidence

Estimators mention STACK/OnScreen takeoff, Excel cost systems and assembly-based estimating products; QuickBooks/accounting and shared calendars are common in broader contractor discussions, but masonry-specific adoption evidence is limited. MCAA and IMI provide estimating, business/safety and technical detail resources. Procore is a GC-oriented project platform, and specialty subs may use it when required by the GC; it does not remove the need for estimator-specific assemblies. Public package pricing was not verified for the commonly mentioned takeoff/estimating products in this review. The $39/month anecdote is a user's report for one product, not a reference WTP. Software complaints center on initial assembly setup and adapting broad takeoff tools to unit/productivity calculations; source sample is small and self-selected.

## Evidence, disagreements, and uncertainty

Masonry spans construction and repair models that have different estimate and scheduling requirements. Anonymous estimator posts identify concrete examples of takeoff/productivity workflows but do not establish typical software use. MCAA and IMI offer trade-specific materials; neither dictates what a CRM must store. Price evidence is sparse and one anecdotal low-cost takeoff price is not evidence of broader software WTP. More interviews across one-person restoration firms and commercial wall subs are needed (medium confidence).

## Handoff to a future pack implementer

Prototype a repair intake with photos/site visit and a plan-based estimate workflow using tenant-configurable material/assembly templates, staged field checks and change records. Ask type of masonry, project segment, takeoff method, crew count, scaffold/equipment needs, inspection requirements and whether materials are stocked or job-purchased. Keep drawings/design, product details, safety and code controls customizable. Validate whether generic line items support unit conversion/assembly costing before claiming a core gap.
