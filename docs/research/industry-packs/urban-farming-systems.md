## Executive summary

**Observed evidence:** Urban farming system providers may design, build, install, commission, and support hydroponic, vertical, rooftop, indoor, container, or building-integrated growing systems. Commercial applications range from controlled-environment food production to an office/hotel amenity with ongoing planting and maintenance. Site/crop/throughput/utility assumptions and ability of the customer to operate the system shape design ([Just Vertical Commercial](https://commercial.justvertical.com/pages/our-services), [AGEYE](https://ageyetech.com/turnkey-farms), [MicroHabitat](https://www.microhabitat.com/indoor-farm)).

**Inference:** For the candidate business, manage a bespoke installation/project and possibly recurring maintenance, with records for site, system components, crop protocol, commissioning, training, utility/water setup, and warranty. This profile excludes operators whose main business is growing/selling produce. “Urban farming systems” remains broad and could include residential garden setups, commercial farm equipment, or living building amenities; use onboarding to branch workflow. Evidence is limited on stable provider economics and software behavior, so status is partial.

## Business model and customer segments

- Customers may include restaurants/hotels, schools, corporate offices, property developers, nonprofits/community organizations, retailers, and aspiring/commercial growers. Buyer can be building owner/facilities, sustainability/amenity manager, or farm operator.
- Project scopes include site feasibility/design, growing racks/towers, irrigation/nutrient loops, lighting/environment controls, rooftop greenhouse/green roof integration, modular/container farm, installation, startup, operator training, repair, crop/operations consulting, and managed planting/maintenance. A building amenity service may install then return weekly; a production system provider may perform commissioning and support without operating the farm.
- Triggers include a new building or vacant rooftop, sustainability/tenant amenity program, community food access, local production, educational program, or commercial farm expansion. Commercial indoor farm economics and building-integration projects vary widely and may be sensitive to utility and market conditions.
- Company structures range from designers/integrators and equipment vendors to fully managed service providers and farm operators. A source example differentiates an equipment/design business from a fully managed farm amenity; another describes turnkey production from seed to market. Do not assume a single service model.

## Terminology and durable records

- Terms: urban agriculture, controlled-environment agriculture (CEA), vertical farm, hydroponics, aquaponics, aeroponics, rooftop greenhouse, grow rack, nutrient solution, irrigation/recirculation, crop plan, throughput, commissioning, MEP coordination, and site readiness.
- Site data includes building/room/roof, structural capacity/engineering, access/loading/elevator, sunlight, water supply/drainage, power/lighting, HVAC/temperature/humidity, floor layout, neighboring operations, zoning/permits, intended crop/use, and operating staff.
- Durable assets may include farm/system, rack zones, lighting, pumps/filters, tanks, dosing/irrigation controls, sensors, and equipment serial/warranty. Crop batch/planting and nutrient measurements are operational records, not necessarily permanent customer assets. Distinguish customer-owned system installed by vendor from a vendor-owned managed service.
- Sensitive/regulated data may include facility plans, access/security, water/chemical use, food-safety records, and commercial crop/production data. Structural, electrical, water and food handling requirements are jurisdiction- and crop-specific.

## Services, intake, quoting, and booking

- Job types: feasibility/site assessment, system design, equipment/build fabrication, installation, utility/MEP coordination, commissioning, training, crop/operations advice, recurring maintenance, planting/rotation, breakdown/repair, and expansion/reconfiguration.
- Intake should identify goal (produce, amenity, teaching, research), crop types/target production, building and site type, area/light, structural/load/roof access, water/power/HVAC, staffing capability, intended ownership/operation, budget/throughput, desired opening date, and whether ongoing management is requested. Field survey/engineering likely needed for rooftop and commercial scale.
- Quoting is likely project-specific and may combine equipment, design/engineering, site readiness, installation, integration, commissioning, operator training, and maintenance/operations contract. AGEYE explicitly describes designing around crop mix, throughput, labor, utilities, building, and operating assumptions; prices are quote-led. No general rate observed.
- Booking is a multi-stage project tied to building works/permits/equipment supply and crop/season timing. Managed amenity maintenance may repeat weekly or seasonally; production installations are one-time with support. Website should route installation-vs-managed-farm scope to consultation.

## Pricing and commercial model

- Evidence-supported quote factors include system size/configuration, crop and throughput, lighting/electrical load, HVAC/environment controls, water/nutrient system, building/roof and access, engineering/MEP, installation, training, commissioning, and ongoing operating responsibilities. Retail/residential hobby kits differ from commercial systems.
- Provider models vary: equipment sales/install, turnkey build, consulting/commissioning, lease/managed farm, and bundled weekly maintenance. MicroHabitat advertises a managed indoor-farm service with weekly maintenance/planting/seasonal rotations; this is a provider-specific commercial amenity, not universal recurrence.
- No reliable service price or software subscription purchase/WTP evidence found. Do not use farm project costs or produce value to infer software WTP.

## Recurrence, scheduling, dispatch, and routing

- Build/install is project-based. Maintenance may be daily/weekly for living amenity installations, scheduled preventive checks for commercial systems, or crop-cycle-based; exact service depends on contract/system. Farm operating tasks are distinct from the installer's maintenance agreement.
- Capacity and schedule constraints include engineered/site-ready building, trades, equipment delivery, utilities commissioning, crop planting/harvest windows, technician skill, water/electric availability, and harvest/retail demand where vendor also operates production.
- Rescheduling an install due to incomplete building/utility work can affect commissioning and planting; recurring service misses can affect crops. Define customer responsibilities, alert/escalation and downtime plans.

## Field workflow, safety, completion, and rework

- Project sequence: discovery/crop economics → site assessment/engineering → system/farm design and utility plan → permits/building readiness → install racks/equipment and plumbing/electrical/environment controls → test water flow, sensors, lighting, and controls → seed/plant trial if included → train site team → hand over system records and support plan. Managed service adds repeated inspection, planting/rotation, cleaning, and issue handling.
- Evidence may include engineered drawings, component/serial list, plumbing/electrical as-built, commissioning readings, calibration settings, water/nutrient logs, crop protocol, training, and acceptance. These records depend on whether the provider is equipment installer or operator.
- Hazards include rooftop height/loading, electrical/water proximity, wet floors, lifting, chemical nutrients/cleaners, food sanitation, allergens, and building egress. Local permitting, structural engineering, food safety, water reuse/discharge, and worker-safety rules require project/location review; do not assume every urban farm is subject to identical rules.
- Noncompletion causes could include inadequate load capacity, poor water/power/HVAC, delayed construction, crop/system mismatch, equipment lead time, commissioning issue, or customer staffing/training gap. This is an inferred project-specific list, not a standard source taxonomy.

## Customer communication and self-service

- Before: clarify system intent and commercial assumptions, site prerequisite checklist, responsibilities, permit/engineering status, timeline, and what the vendor operates. During: milestone updates, construction/utility dependencies, commissioning results, and change approval. After: operating/training manuals, service schedules, crop/maintenance reminders, warranty, and support escalation.
- Customer can submit site photos/plans and goals, but a technical assessment should validate system feasibility. A portal may be helpful for multi-site property and production clients but adoption is not evidenced.
- Do not automate a yield/profit promise, permit clearance, or building-load approval. These need qualified human review and assumptions written down.

## Equipment, inventory, suppliers, and workforce

- System components may include structural racks, lighting, irrigation, reservoirs, pumps, filters, injectors, sensors, climate control, controls/software, seed/seedlings, growing media, nutrients, packaging, and cleaning supplies. Configuration is crop/system-specific.
- Roles may include system designer/agronomist, structural engineer, electrician/plumber/HVAC, installers, commissioning technician, grower/operator, and recurring maintenance technician. Fully managed services supply horticulture labor that install-only firms may not.
- Equipment and consumables inventory is relevant to farm operators and managed maintenance, less relevant to one-time integrators. Traceability and calibration records may be useful for food or production operations, but evidence does not establish one universal compliance scheme.

## Reporting and operating measures

- Vendors mention profitability, throughput, crop protocols, and system performance but no shared service-business KPIs. Candidate install measures (inference): milestone variance, commissioning pass/fail, time to first crop, warranty callbacks, training completion, and maintenance response. Farm operator metrics (yield, energy/water per output, waste, crop loss, revenue) should only appear if provider operates the farm or customer wants operations support.
- Separate installer delivery performance from crop/farm performance. Segment by customer, site/system, crop, technology, scope, service contract, and season. No benchmark values supported.

## Existing software and operator evidence

- Vertical-farm vendors describe system/production software or complete farm operations, but this research did not find a software suite standard for small system installers. Providers also use vendors for controls/automation, engineering design tools, accounting, and maintenance; common stack claims remain unsupported.
- A first-person small business discussion describes greenhouse fabrication/installation and automated irrigation as possible service areas, but the author is exploring options rather than reporting a mature firm's operating stack. Urban farming forum posts show debate about operating/economic models: small garden/education/community projects vs commercial vertical farm economics. Use as context, not complaint prevalence.
- No verified software price, operator purchase, CRM complaint, migration concern, or software WTP statement found. Some automated system providers imply equipment control software bundled with installed system; that is not evidence of CRM purchase.

## Evidence, disagreements, and uncertainty

Sources support multiple non-equivalent meanings: a building amenity with weekly planting, a commercially productive controlled-environment farm, and a turnkey system installer. Their recurring service and asset records differ. Providers argue for business-case/throughput planning, while community discussions include projects whose aims are education, food access, or a hobby; economic viability is not assumed. Regulatory scope depends on rooftop/indoor structure, produce use, and jurisdiction. Software usage and WTP are unknown; status remains partial.

## Handoff to a future pack implementer

Prototype project intake that classifies residential/amenity/commercial production use, system/site type, crop/throughput, utility readiness, engineering/trade coordination, commissioning and training. Offer recurring maintenance only as an explicit tenant-selected scope; keep crop analytics/production reporting separate from install project performance. Ask whether the company sells systems, installs them, manages crops, or operates farms, and whether it serves rooftops, indoor spaces, containers, or hobby gardens. Keep all structural, food-safety, utility, crop, and service requirements site-configurable.
