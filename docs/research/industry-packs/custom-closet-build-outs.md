## Executive summary

Custom closet build-outs are usually design-and-install projects for walk-in/reach-in closets, pantries, linen storage, mudrooms, wardrobes and related home storage. The operator measures and designs a space, presents material/configuration options, prices parts and labor, orders/cuts components, schedules installation and resolves fit/finish punch items. Closet dealer/manufacturer workflow differs from general finish carpentry: component catalogs, room clearances, standardized systems and often design visualization/cut lists form the sales-to-production bridge.

Closet design software vendor Pro100 describes a single design file producing pricing, elevations and part lists; ClosetPro markets customer proposals and manufacturer/CAM export. These vendor descriptions identify workflow needs but do not prove every small installer uses design/CAM software. Owner and consumer accounts describe measurement, custom-vs-kit quality/price tradeoffs, installation delays, missing parts and follow-up complaints. A pack should emphasize consultation, room/opening measurements, versioned design/selections, ordered components, crew handoff and punch. Keep `Closet build out` separate because it appears under Niche carpentry, a broader carpentry context that may involve bespoke one-off construction rather than closet-system dealership.

## Business model and customer segments

Residential homeowners are primary buyers; builders/developers, interior designers, organizers, property managers and real-estate/staging professionals can provide referrals or repeat work. Work includes walk-in and reach-in systems, pantry/linen closets, wardrobe inserts, mudrooms, drawers, shoe storage, shelves, rods, lighting and accessories. Some firms sell modular systems with installation; others design/build custom cabinets or install customer/dealer-supplied kits. New construction builder volume differs from remodeler/homeowner consultation and retrofit work.

Projects vary from a single closet to multiple spaces throughout a new home. Dealer/design companies may separate in-home designer, measurement verifier, shop/manufacturing, delivery and installer. Independent carpenters may take design, build and install themselves. Franchises/dealer territories vary; employees versus subcontracted installers change quality and capacity management.

## Terminology and durable records

Capture customer, property, room/closet, opening/wall dimensions, ceiling/floor conditions, layout/design revision, system/material, component/part, finish, accessory, order, cut list, installer, punch and warranty. Site context includes room type, wall condition, out-of-square/sloped ceiling, door swing, access, existing shelving/removal, electrical/lighting and occupant preparation. Room dimensions can be retained for repeat/add-on work; measurements and product selections should remain tied to the design revision and installation job. Keep photos, annotated measurements, rendered design/elevations, quote, signed change, supplier confirmation, part list and final fit photos.

## Services, intake, quoting, and booking

Intake asks closet/room type, dimensions or measurement visit, desired storage and finish, budget range if volunteered, current system/removal, ceiling/wall/door constraints, drawers/lighting/accessories, home access and schedule. Design consultation and precise field measure are common; instant web pricing is useful only for standardized ranges or lead qualification. Quotes can be system tiers, per component/part, room package or custom labor/material, with delivery, removal, wall repair and lighting/extras separate. Preserve choices and excluded wall/paint/electrical work.

Flow: lead/referral → consult/measure → design/options → price/approval/deposit → production/order and delivery verification → install scheduling → site protection/install → adjustments/punch → final payment/warranty. A design change must update estimate, components and order before cut/production. Missing parts or incorrect measurements create return visits; route the correction to the responsible design, supply or installation step. Manufacturer/dealer production lead time is distinct from installation date.

## Pricing and commercial model

Inputs include system vs custom construction, linear wall length/height, number of sections, material/finish, drawer/door count, hardware, accessories, ceiling/angle, removal, delivery, install complexity, travel, wall repair and electrical work. Deposit may fund custom orders; final balance follows install/punch. Changes after order may create restocking or remake costs. A Houzz consumer comparison gives one historical example of custom closet company quotes ($8k–$12k) versus a selected independent carpenter at about $3.5k; this is a single localized consumer report, not a market price or software WTP signal.

Pro100's vendor page states a one-time license model rather than subscription, while ClosetPro offers a different closet-design package; current numeric pricing was not verified. A shop-owner forum post about closet CAD reports a $1,500 closet module purchase and $20,000 custom programming over a year to reach a usable manufacturing system. This dated anecdote is about specialized production software and a specific shop, not a typical CRM budget. It shows why integration/setup cost and fit matter; actual WTP remains uncertain.

## Recurrence, scheduling, dispatch, and routing

Sales are one-time, but builders, designers and homeowners can generate multiple rooms/additions. Schedule consultation, measure verification, ordered components, delivery, install and punch separately. Match crew to system/product, room access, stairs and lifting needs. Backorder, missing/damaged parts, measurement mismatch, site not ready, wall condition, design revision or installer capacity can delay. Document which milestone is blocked and who must act; do not promise install before order confirmation.

## Field workflow, safety, completion, and rework

Verify room and approved revision, wall conditions, dimensions, components and access; protect floors/contents; locate framing/appropriate attachment points per system; install rail/standards/carcasses/drawers/accessories; check level/plumb/fit/clearance and operation; clean and photograph; resolve punch and obtain acceptance. Evidence can include wall/measurement photos, component count, anchor/rail location notes, finish damage, drawer/door operation, design changes, missing parts and completed punch. Anchoring and anti-tip requirements depend on system/manufacturer and substrate. Renovation may involve lead paint in older homes; applicable OSHA/EPA rules and contractor licensing vary. Rework includes wrong dimensions, cut error, out-of-square walls, missing pieces, mismatched finish, drawer/door binding and site damage.

## Customer communication and self-service

Customers need consultation booking, design approval, price/options, order/lead-time, room preparation, installer arrival, changed scope, punch return, warranty and care guidance. Portal approval can record choices and changes; final measurements and structural installation conditions should be checked by staff. Consumer sources report contrasting satisfaction and complaints about missing parts, appointment delay and poor follow-up; these are isolated reviews, not prevalence.

## Equipment, inventory, suppliers, and workforce

Parts include panels, rods, standards, brackets, drawer boxes/slides, doors, pulls, baskets, lighting and fasteners. Dealers may stock samples or components; producers order/cut to job. Tools include measurement/laser, saw/drill, level and design/CAD/CAM systems. Workforce can include designer/consultant, field measurer, fabricator, delivery, lead installer and helper. Keep supplier/catalog, finish and part identifiers synchronized across approved drawings, order and field kit. Employee and subcontractor setup varies; time/job cost is useful for install-heavy or multi-crew businesses.

## Reporting and operating measures

Inferred useful measures include referral lead conversion, consult-to-design and design-to-order time, revision count, material/order variance, install-on-time, first-visit completion, missing-part/remake rate, punch closure time, gross margin per room/system and callback rate by installer/product. Segment by project type, system/custom, lead source, designer, installer and builder account. Vendor claims about conversion impact from visualization are not treated as verified outcomes.

## Existing software and operator evidence

Design software is a clear specialist category: Pro100 describes room design, pricing, elevations, parts and CAM handoff; ClosetPro describes customizable pricing, proposals and CAM export. General CRM/field-service apps may manage lead, schedule, communication and invoice, but could leave drawings/cut lists and order state outside. Woodworking forum participants report support/usability friction and expensive custom setup for one closet manufacturing shop; user sample is small and dated. Consumer accounts reveal service delivery variations, and pricing comparisons show customers may choose a local carpenter instead of branded closet dealer. Current CRM-specific operator WTP remains low confidence.

## Evidence, disagreements, and uncertainty

Sources show three models: modular closet dealer, custom cabinet/closet shop and independent installer. They differ in design and manufacturing integration. Vendor claims about conversion, pricing and savings are promotional; consumer quotes are anecdotal and regional. No independent representative CRM WTP was found. Confidence is medium for workflow, low for prevalence and software spend.

## Handoff to a future pack implementer

Prototype consult, room measure, design/version approval, component order, delivery check, install and punch. Ask whether the company is a dealer, shop manufacturer or installer, and which design/CAD/CAM systems are already in use. Keep catalog, anchoring and manufacturer details tenant-specific. Validate the design-software handoff before proposing new product capability; preserve the separate Niche carpentry `Closet build out` candidate.
