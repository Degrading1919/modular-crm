## Executive summary

**Observed evidence:** Carpet cleaners provide in-home or on-site maintenance/deep cleaning for wall-to-wall carpet, area rugs, upholstery and sometimes tile/grout or related surfaces. Residential customers include owners, tenants and property managers; commercial work includes offices, restaurants, schools and other facilities, often scheduled out of hours or under repeat contracts. The operational loop is qualify surface/condition → estimate by area/rooms and condition → book route → pre-inspect/fiber-test and protect → clean using compatible method → groom/dry and document → collect payment → follow-up/maintenance. It is a mobile cleaning service, distinct from flooring installation/refinishing.

A useful pack emphasizes rooms/surfaces, fibers/condition, stain/odor concerns, access, prep, chosen method, customer consent, dry-time expectations and before/after proof. Repeat frequency is customer/property-driven, not a universal subscription. IICRC S100 includes inspection, chemistry, equipment, methods, safety, residential cleaning, rugs and administrative procedure; CRI tests cleaners/equipment for soil removal, resoiling, residual moisture and appearance retention. No unsupported health/remediation promise should be automated.

## Business model and customer segments

Residential jobs can be whole-home or selected rooms, move-in/out, pet/stain response and scheduled maintenance. Commercial jobs may be offices, restaurants, schools, hotels and property-management portfolios, with after-hours access, zones, recurring schedules, proof and purchase-order requirements. Lead sources include local search/reviews, referrals, property managers, realtors, cleaning/floor stores and repeat customers. Purchase triggers include visible soil/spills, pets/odor, tenancy turnover, event/season prep, warranty maintenance and facility contract. Demand may be seasonal around moves/holidays, but local climate and customer base matter.

Operator structures range from solo owner-operator with portable equipment and a helper to truckmount vans, multiple crews, rug pickup/plant cleaning, commercial sales and call-taking/dispatch. A carpet cleaner can diversify into upholstery, tile/grout, water extraction or restoration, but these require different equipment, handling and workflow. Cleaning is not carpet/floor installation, sanding or refinishing.

## Terminology and durable records

Terms include client/property, room/area, carpet type/fiber/construction, pile, rug, upholstery item, soil/stain, spot, pre-inspection, pre-vacuum, pre-spray, extraction, low-moisture/encapsulation, bonnet, rinse, protector, grooming, dry time and post-inspection. Location data can include address, floor/room access, elevator/parking, water/power availability, building access, pets, furniture-moving expectations, occupant sensitivities and preferred arrival window.

Persistent site history may include flooring material/fiber, known cleaning constraints, prior treatments and recurring commercial zone schedule. A job should hold room dimensions/approximate area, condition, stain map/photo, test results, cleaning method, chemical/product and dilution as relevant, furniture movement, protector/add-ons, pre-existing damage, before/after images and acceptance. These one-job facts should not be presented as permanent floor condition. Rug pickup workflows may need custody/tags, item dimensions, fringe/colorfastness, cleaning method, storage and delivery status. Protect codes and household photos; cleaning notes about pets or occupant health should be private/minimized.

## Services, intake, quoting, and booking

Common jobs include hot-water extraction (often called steam cleaning although water is heated/extracted, not necessarily steam), low-moisture/encapsulation, spot treatment, pet odor/stain treatment, protector, upholstery, area rugs, stairs, commercial carpet, tile/grout and emergency water extraction if the firm offers it. Not every cleaner offers all methods, restoration or guaranteed stain removal. Intake should capture room/area/count and approximate size, carpet/rug/fiber if known, visible soil/stains/odor, pets, prior products, desired service/add-ons, photos, occupancy, parking/water access, furniture-moving scope, date flexibility and whether site visit is needed. Commercial quote may need floor plan, square footage/zones, access/security, operating hours, tender/PO and repeat frequency.

Price may be menu/room, measured square-foot, stair/item count, site estimate or contract bid. Inputs include area/rooms, carpet condition, furniture, stairs, fiber/specialty rug, soil/pet treatment, protector, pre-vacuum, drying constraints, access/travel, minimum charge, out-of-hours window and commercial frequency. Quote should state what's included, furniture limitations, stain results not guaranteed, dry-time guidance and any extra work authorization. A form may give a preliminary range; unusual rug, severe contamination, disputed damage or commercial tender should be reviewed by a human.

## Pricing and commercial model

Residential service often collects payment at completion; commercial/property-manager agreements can be invoiced by site/PO, recurring schedule or net terms. Deposits are not an evidence-backed universal default. Cancellation/rescheduling terms, trip charges and stain treatment warranty vary by company. A CRI consumer page recommends professional deep cleaning every 12–18 months and notes manufacturer warranty can make certified products relevant; it is consumer guidance, not a binding schedule for every household.

## Recurrence, scheduling, dispatch, and routing

Residential cleanings are usually one-time or repeat after months; commercial contracts may be nightly, weekly, monthly or periodic deep clean by zone. Avoid imposing a fixed annual renewal on all residents. Route density, job duration, truckmount/portable equipment, stairs/elevator, water/power, parking, drying time, occupancy/furniture preparation and out-of-hours access shape booking. Some work fits a service window; commercial jobs may be scheduled for vacant hours. Assign equipment/crew capacity and avoid overlapping use of a truckmount or rug plant. Calls may add rooms/items at arrival; record customer approval for extras. Weather/humidity can affect dry-time expectations; avoid guaranteeing same duration across fiber, ventilation and soil.

## Field workflow, safety, completion, and rework

Inquiry/photos → scope and rough quote → confirm access/prep → arrival/site walk and pre-inspection → identify fiber/condition, colorfastness or stability risk and pre-existing damage → explain limitations/obtain approval → protect furnishings and area → vacuum/spot/precondition → selected cleaning method with equipment/product compatible with surface → extraction/rinse as method calls for → groom/set drying guidance → post-inspect and compare → customer acceptance/photos → invoice/collect → drying or stain follow-up.

IICRC S100 describes pre/post inspections, chemistry, equipment, methods and safety. CRI's Seal of Approval testing evaluates product/equipment performance including soil removal, resoiling, appearance and residual moisture; certification is a signal, not proof for every carpet condition. Follow chemical label/SDS, PPE, ventilation, electrical/water controls and equipment safety. Never promise to remove every stain or claim sanitization/health effect beyond product label and evidence. Escalate suspected asbestos/contamination, sewage or structural water damage to appropriately qualified remediation rather than ordinary cleaning. Noncompletion reasons include no access, furniture not moved, unsafe/electrical/wet area, unknown fiber/unstable dye, pre-existing damage risk, equipment failure, no water/power, customer declines revised scope or extreme contamination. Record photos, reason, customer notice, charge/trip policy, return/dry-time plan and disputed areas.

## Customer communication and self-service

Before visit: room/item scope, furniture prep, parking/access, pets/occupancy, approximate arrival, method and dry-time expectation. During: pre-existing damage/stain limitations, changes and any extra charge approval. After: care/drying guidance, treated areas, product restrictions and receipt, plus optional reminder based on customer preference. Portal can request rooms/photos, approve an estimate/add-on, pick a window, pay and request callback. Do not automate stain removal or health promises, recommend unsafe mixing/chemical use, or say an area is ready for use absent job conditions. SMS/email consent and local rules apply.

## Equipment, inventory, suppliers, and workforce

Equipment varies by method and scale: truckmount or portable extractor, vacuum, agitation tools, hoses/wands, air movers, moisture meter, spot tools, rug dusting/wash/dry facilities, upholstery tools, PPE and vehicle. Supplies include prespray/rinse/spotter/deodorizer/protector, pads/brushes, hose/filters and containment. Product compatibility, dilution, SDS, expiry and vehicle stock matter; lot tracking might matter for a large/commercial or incident-sensitive operator but should not be forced on a solo cleaner. Rug pickup creates tagged custody/delivery chain. Roles include estimator/call taker, cleaner/technician, helper, rug plant staff and commercial account manager. Employees need training on fiber/chemicals/equipment; certifications and insurance depend on firm/contract. Track hours, travel and job duration as staffing grows; commission/add-on policies are company-specific.

## Reporting and operating measures

Useful measures include lead source/quote conversion, average ticket/room, revenue and margin by method/add-on, route revenue per van/crew day, job minutes versus estimate, repeat interval/retention, commercial contract profitability, stain callback/rework, refund/complaint rate, drying complaint and chemical/material use. Definitions vary; do not overfit dashboards to assumed universal room rate. Jobber markets quotes, job scheduling, dispatch and invoicing; Workiz markets crew assignment, traffic-aware planning, add-on proposals and payments. These features suggest operators value pipeline, utilization and average-ticket visibility, but vendor claims do not prove KPI adoption.

## Evidence, disagreements, and uncertainty

IICRC and CRI cover quality/safety, while operator posts show quoting and preparation questions; Workiz/Jobber market efficiency and upselling. The latter can conflict with conservative customer education if “good-better-best” options create unnecessary treatments. EPA general chemical and IAQ guidance supports caution but does not set carpet-cleaning protocols. Carpet/rug material, method, customer prep, commercial schedule and restoration capability vary. Confidence is high on pre/post inspection, scope and surface constraints; medium on common addons/records; low on stack prevalence, commercial repeat rates and software WTP.

## Handoff to a future pack implementer

Prototype room/surface quote, prep/access, method-specific checklist, before/after evidence and optional repeat reminder. Ask about residential/commercial split, rugs/upholstery, methods, truckmount/portable, commercial contracts and restoration work. Keep product/fiber rules, rates, furniture moving, staining guarantees, chemical choices, scheduling windows and recurrence tenant-configurable. Keep carpet cleaning separate from install/refinishing and keep water damage restoration separate unless offered. Open questions: whether structured room/asset history adds enough value over job notes, how often account-driven recurrence should be recommended, and actual software subscription budgets for solo versus multi-crew firms.
