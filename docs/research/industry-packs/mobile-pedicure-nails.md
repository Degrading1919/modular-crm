## Executive summary

**Observed evidence:** Mobile nail businesses bring tools/products to a private home, hotel, workplace, care setting, or event. A tech may provide portable dry manicure/pedicure or carry a basin; foot-spa choices drive water, waste, disinfection/contact-time, and documentation burden. Texas requires cleaning/disinfecting foot basins after each client and retains written records (60 days in its guidance); EPA recommends an EPA-registered hospital disinfectant according to the label, and advises not soaking feet with open sores/skin barrier injury. OSHA details chemical exposure, ventilation and labeling hazards for nail workers. First-person mobile techs report concerns about carrying equipment, booking deposits, travel fees and personal safety; a nail-salon owner discussion stresses contraindication forms, sanitation protocol and time for reset.

**Inference:** A strong pack needs to ask whether service is in a licensed mobile unit or at client premises, which nail services are offered, whether water/footbath is used, and which local rules apply. It should represent sanitation as an ordered service record (tool/footbath type, cleaning/disinfectant, time, technician, date, exception) while allowing a dry/no-circulation setup. Never default to medical pedicure, treat infection/ingrown nails, or assume a mobile visit is lawful merely because an individual nail-tech license is held.

## Business model and customer segments

Customers include convenience-seeking consumers, older/homebound clients, wedding/event groups, workplaces, hotels, and people preferring private one-on-one appointments. Typical menu includes manicure, polish change, gel/acrylic/dip/nail art, basic pedicure, add-ons, and group/event blocks depending on license/training. Operators can be solo licensed nail technicians, a mobile salon van, or a small team. Repeat maintenance/rebooking can be frequent, but cadence and service interval depend on product/nail growth. Home calls may serve accessibility but require carrying gear; group events may make route travel economical. Ordinary cosmetic nail care is distinct from podiatric/medical care.

## Terminology and durable records

Use customer, service location, mobile unit, nail tech, manicure/pedicure, enhancement, foot basin/spa, reusable tool, single-use item, sanitation log, and rebook. Location records: address/parking/access, table/chair/workspace, lighting, ventilation/windows, electricity, water, pets/children, event host, and service radius. Persistent equipment includes portable table, basin/footspa and powered nail drill; retain maintenance/model and sanitation history where reusable. Customer preferences may include polish/gel shade, product sensitivity/allergy, style photos, prior product/extension, and contraindication prompts. Keep skin/health notes private and limited to what a licensee may properly ask and use. Do not record a diagnosis or represent nail care as treatment.

## Services, intake, quoting, and booking

Offer only services allowed by license and training: basic manicure/pedicure, polish/gel, enhancements, soak-off, nail art, event/group service, and travel setup. Intake: requested service/products/removal/art, number of people, event date/location, address and parking, setup/ventilation/light/power/water, foot basin/spa preference, mobility/access, relevant sensitivity or visible condition needing professional review, and travel/booking policy. Ask for reference images for complex nail art and allow quote review for length, repairs, removals or multiple designs. Disclose what the client must provide (chair/space/outlet) and cancellation/deposit. Do not proceed with an infected/open wound/skin condition or requests beyond scope; refer to appropriate licensed health professional. In Texas, the mobile establishment is a licensed self-contained vehicle and cannot perform regulated service inside a client's home; other state pathways differ. Confirm city permits and mobile practice legality before booking.

## Pricing and commercial model

Price variables: service type and duration, gel/acrylic removal, nail length/repair/art complexity, foot soak vs dry pedicure, products, group size, travel zone/time, setup/breakdown, minimum/deposit, and after-hours event. Local salon menu prices do not establish mobile fees. A Tampa-area mobile service advertises basic pedicure and home-visit menu but prices should be checked directly; a separate mobile-tech Reddit inquiry asks how to set travel rates and a 2026 mobile nail tech reports considering booking deposits and a travel charge. These sources show pricing questions, not price consensus. **Software willingness-to-pay:** no mobile nail-specific subscription purchase/WTP evidence found. Vagaro lists US $23.99/month base + $10 for additional bookable calendars (limited-time price); GlossGenius lists $24/$48/$148 monthly when paid annually. These are beauty-software list prices and not evidence a mobile nail operator buys them. Avoid deriving software budget from nail service prices.

## Recurrence, scheduling, dispatch, and routing

Appointments need time for service, product cure, travel, set-up, footbath cleaning/disinfection, reset and packing. Rebooking cadence can be suggested by service/product but should be customer- and technician-configurable. Route by service zone and setup carrying capacity; a long service plus travel can sharply reduce daily visits. Group/event bookings require multiple technicians, manicure/pedicure sequence, stations and sanitation between clients. Water/power availability, room ventilation, pets/children, parking, late clients, unsafe space, and customer preference for basin vs dry service can alter schedule. Keep a buffer for required disinfectant contact time; an automation must not promise the next start sooner than the operator's rule allows.

## Field workflow, safety, completion, and rework

Before confirming: verify nail license and whether service location/model is permitted; capture exact menu, client count, requested art/removal, setup/ventilation/water, travel/deposit and screening issue. At site: confirm identity/service, inspect whether client condition is within cosmetic scope, set up clean station with ventilation appropriate to products, separate clean from used tools, wash hands, clean/disinfect/sterilize reusable implements according to local rule, dispose of single-use files/applicators as required, and record pedicure basin/spa steps. EPA says to drain/remove debris, clean, disinfect with EPA-registered hospital disinfectant for label contact time, rinse/air-dry; circulating foot spas require the system flushed/circulated. Texas requires after-client basin/spa records and removal from service when sanitation records are missing. Follow local regulation; do not assume a disposable liner substitutes for all basin cleaning or vice versa. Complete service, record products/shade/art and aftercare instructions, clean station, collect payment, and rebook. OSHA identifies chemical vapor/dust exposure, labeling, ventilation and safe work practices; client homes may lack salon-grade source capture. Noncompletion reasons: infection/open wound/contraindication; no legal mobile permission; no water/ventilation/space or safe power; insufficient setup/time; sanitation log/equipment issue; client absent/late; product reaction; requested procedure outside scope; or weather/traffic. Record objective condition and referral/decline without diagnosis. Rework is usually cosmetic repair under provider policy, not medical remedy.

## Customer communication and self-service

Communicate exact menu, service length, price/travel/deposit, prep (e.g. remove old polish or disclose enhancement), required room/chair/ventilation, technician identity, arrival window, delays, service restriction/refusal policy, aftercare and rebooking. Portal may show upcoming appointment, shade/art preferences, receipts and reschedule. Health screening should be protected; do not distribute foot/skin-condition information in appointment reminders or team messages. Human review is essential for wounds, suspected infection, allergies, enhancement removal damage, local legality, and whether a site can be safely ventilated.

## Equipment, inventory, suppliers, and workforce

Portable nail table/chair, lamps, drill/files, polish/gel/acrylic, removers, single-use files/buffers, disinfectant jars, EPA-registered disinfectant, hand hygiene, PPE, ventilation/dust collector, towel/basin/footspa, liners, clean/used laundry bags, waste containers, extension cords and portable water supply may be needed. Equipment choice drives mobile weight, setup, chemical exposure and recordkeeping. Separate clean/used tools and salon products; track lot/expiry only where practitioner policy warrants. Teams need state license verification, sanitation training, service skills, travel/time, chemical handling, table cleanup and possible commission/tips/payroll. In a private home, safety/entry check-in may matter; exact policy should reflect operator insurance and risk assessment.

## Reporting and operating measures

Inferred measures: appointments/services by type, rebook rate, revenue and technician time, drive/setup/cleanup share, travel fee realization, group revenue, cancellations/no-shows, design add-on rate, customer satisfaction/correction, chemical/product use, foot-spa cleaning record completeness, and sanitation issue/removal from service. Some sanitation records are direct regulatory records in example jurisdictions, whereas commercial KPIs are inferred. Avoid public-facing rankings based on client health data.

## Evidence, disagreements, and uncertainty

Mobile nail work ranges from dry nail-art service to water-based pedicure and licensed mobile van; the latter creates different sanitation and utility duties. EPA guidance is federal best-practice guidance for foot spas, not a complete substitute for state board requirements; Texas is a concrete state example. OSHA chemical/respiratory obligations depend on employer/exposure circumstances. Some online operator replies make broad licensing claims; do not rely on those over current state board rules. Demand segments (homebound vs convenience/luxury/events), acceptable travel fee and product/service margins are not established. Medical pedicure and treatment of infection should remain outside this candidate pack absent the separate clinical qualifications/legal scope.

## Handoff to a future pack implementer

Ask state/city and legal mobile model, allowed services, home/unit setting, footbath vs dry process, group size, travel area, setup/ventilation/water, booking fee/deposit, technician count, and whether sanitation records are contractually or legally required. Prototype service menu and mobile-location readiness, customer preference plus private screening handoff, travel/setup/reset buffers, reusable tool and footspa sanitation log with localizable steps, decline/referral outcome, and receipt/rebooking. Make a no-basin/dry-pedicure option possible. Validate state/local scope and sanitizer label rules before any default checklist is treated as compliance advice.
